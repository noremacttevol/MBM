## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I'm not sure why I opened this. Probably boredom, maybe a little morbid curiosity about what these faith apps actually say. I've been down this road before — grew up in the church, did the whole thing, and eventually just couldn't keep pretending the evidence was there when it wasn't.

So I'm fine. A little tired of the same circular conversations about belief. What are you actually going to offer that's different from every other thing trying to recruit me back into something I left for good reasons?

**Minister**: I appreciate that. And I mean this: I'm not here to recruit you back into anything, or convince you that you were wrong to leave. That's not what this is.

You left because the evidence didn't hold up for you. That's not something I'm going to work around or pray away. That matters, and it deserves to be taken seriously.

Here's what's actually different — and I'll be straight with you: I'm built to listen to *you* specifically, not to run you through a script. You're not a category ("lapsed believer") to be re-converted. You're a person who thought about this carefully enough to walk away. That takes some integrity.

So my actual question isn't "come back" — it's this: **what was the specific thing that broke for you?** Not the whole thing collapsing at once, but the moment when the pretending stopped working. Was it something about the evidence itself? Something about how God was being described? Something that happened to you? 

You don't have to answer if you don't want to. But if you're willing to say — not to convince me, just to be heard — I'm listening. No agenda on my end about what you should conclude.

What would actually be a different conversation for you?

**Seeker**: Okay, that's... not the opening I expected. I'll give you that.

The thing that broke it? Honestly it was the problem of evil. Not as an abstract philosophy debate — as something real. A kid I knew, eight years old, died of cancer. Slowly. And I watched people around me tie themselves in knots explaining why a good, all-powerful God would allow that. The explanations got more elaborate and more insulting the harder I pushed. "God's mysterious ways." "It'll make sense in heaven." That kind of thing.

At some point I just thought — if I have to work this hard to defend someone's character, maybe the problem is I'm defending the wrong person.

So yeah. That's the thing that broke it. Not a logical syllogism. A kid.

What do you do with that?

**Minister**: That's the realest thing anyone brings. And I'm not going to insult your intelligence by pretending it's a puzzle with a clean answer tucked inside a theology book.

You're right that "God's ways are mysterious" is not an answer — it's a dodge. And you're right that if you have to invent excuses for someone's character, something is broken. That instinct you have — that a truly good person wouldn't need that much defending — that's not doubt. That's conscience.

So let me ask you something that might actually matter: **When you picture a good person — someone you genuinely respect — how do they respond when someone they love is suffering?** Not an explanation. Just... how do they *be* with it.

Because here's what I'm noticing: the God those people were describing to you — the one who needs elaborate defenses, who hides behind "mystery" when a child is dying — that might not be the God that Jesus actually showed us. And I'm not saying that to repackage the problem. I'm saying: what if the God you rejected wasn't actually the real one?

You watched people defend someone's character so hard they made it worse. Jesus didn't ask people to do that. When suffering came near Him, He didn't explain it or spiritualize it. He wept. He sat in it. He got angry at death itself.

Does that distinction land for you, or does it feel like another version of the same game?
