So yeah. That's what's sitting with me, I guess. Not grief exactly — more like frustration at how reflexively people reach for those phrases when they don't actually know anything.

**Minister**: That's real. And you're right to be frustrated with those phrases — they're hollow when what someone actually needs is someone to sit with the weight of what they've lost, not to rush past it with comfort that doesn't actually fit.

A few months in, you're past the initial shock but the person is still gone. And everyone's moved on to their own lives. That isolation after a loss — where people think one conversation and a cliché closes the door — that stings different than the grief itself sometimes.

I won't do that to you. I won't tell you he's in a better place. I don't know that, and you don't either, and you're right to bristle at people speaking with certainty about things they can't actually see.

What I *do* know is that what you're carrying is real. The person mattered. The loss matters. And your frustration at people treating it carelessly — that's not bitterness; that's integrity.

Can I ask — in the quiet moments, what's the hardest part? Is it the loss itself, or is some of it still tangled up with how the people around you handled it?

**Seeker**: Both, honestly. But the thing that keeps nagging at me is — if there were a God who actually cared, why does any of this happen at all? My friend wasn't a bad person. He was better than most people I know. And he died at 38 from pancreatic cancer, in pain, over about four months.

So yeah. It's tangled. The grief is real. But the platitudes made it worse because they expose this gap — people *say* God is good and in control, and then something like this happens and they just... paper over it with phrases. And they seem completely unbothered by the contradiction.

I'm not looking to be converted here, just so we're clear. If this app is going to tell me there's a plan and it all makes sense, I'll close it right now.

**Minister**: I hear you. And I'm going to be straight with you: I'm not going to tell you there's a plan that makes this okay, because I don't know that there is one. Your friend suffered. He died too young. That's not *part* of some beautiful design — it's a real loss, and pretending otherwise is exactly the kind of papering-over you're calling out.

Here's what I *actually* believe, and I'll be honest about why: I think the people who rush to "it's all good" are often more committed to defending their idea of God than to telling the truth about what happened. And that's a real failure on their part.

But I also think there's a difference between "God has a master plan that makes suffering *meaningful*" and "God is with you *in* the suffering." The first one I can't defend, and I won't try. The second one is quieter, and it doesn't erase the pain — but it's different.

Your friend mattered. His life was real. His dying was real. And if there *is* a God who is actually good — not just powerful, but *good* — then that God grieves what happened too. Not because there was some hidden reason for it, but because your friend was a person, and his death diminished the world.

I'm curious about something, though I'll understand if it's too much: Do you *want* there to be a God who's actually good? Or has this all made that feel impossible?
