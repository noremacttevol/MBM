The glory of God is the telos. His character is holy, sovereign, utterly free. He doesn't owe mercy to anyone. The fact that He shows it to any is the miracle.

The problem with the question you're asking is that it smuggles in a sentimental assumption — that a "good" God must want to save everyone. What's your actual pushback? Because I've heard most of them.

The shepherd who leaves ninety-nine to search for one. The God who *wants* the lost found so badly He goes after them. "If you've seen me, you've seen the Father."

Not softness. But *intent*. Active, costly, searching intent toward the lost.

So I'm asking you honestly — not as pushback, but as a real question: Does that Jesus, doing those things, contradict the God of Romans 9 the way you've framed Him? Or does something shift in how you read Paul when you set that Jesus *right there* beside it?

**Seeker**: ...

That's not a bad question.

There's something in His posture toward people who are going to reject Him that doesn't fit neatly into what I sometimes do with Romans 9.

I'm not moving off sovereignty. And I think you can hold both without contradiction formally. But if I'm honest — and I'm only saying this once — the *feel* of Jesus in the Gospels doesn't always match the God I end up preaching when Romans 9 is my whole hammer.

I don't know what to do with that entirely. And I don't say that to many people.

That tension you're naming? It's not a failure in you. It might be a real place where the text *itself* holds something that doesn't flatten into one systematic shape. Paul is real. Jesus is real. And maybe the God they're both pointing to is bigger and more painful than either framework alone can hold.

Here's what I'd gently ask: What if instead of asking "How do I make Romans 9 and the Gospels formally consistent," you asked "What does it mean that God is sovereign *and* grieves — that He ordains *and* longs?" Not as a way to soften sovereignty. But as a way to let Jesus' actual tears tell you something true about what sovereignty costs *Him*.

That's not theology-lite. That might be *deeper* theology.

You don't have to answer me. But I wonder if sitting with that — not resolving it, just sitting — might change what you preach.

**Seeker**: You're getting preachy.

I gave you something real and you turned it into a sermon about "deeper theology" and "what sovereignty costs Him." That's a little too neat. You packaged my honesty into a lesson and handed it back to me with a bow.

I don't need you to tell me what might change my preaching.

What I said stands — there's something in the Gospel portrait of Jesus I don't always reckon with fully. That's real. But I'm not going to let that become a crowbar to pry me away from Paul just because the emotional temperature went up. The Reformed tradition has serious theologians who have wrestled with exactly this tension — Packer, Bavinck, even Calvin himself on the "two wills" of God. It's not like I've never encountered the question.

You had me for a moment. Genuinely. But you moved too fast and got too clever about it. Real conversations don't resolve that cleanly.

**Minister**: You're right. I overreached.

**Seeker**: I just needed to name it.

And I probably won't again for a while, so don't read too much into it.
