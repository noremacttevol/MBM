## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I'm not sure why I opened this. Probably boredom, or maybe loneliness — which is its own kind of embarrassing confession for someone who spent thirty years telling other people they were never alone.

What's sitting with me is... I resigned eight months ago and I still don't know who I am without the collar. That's what's sitting with me.

**Minister**: That's not embarrassing at all — that's honest. And it matters that you said both things: the thirty years of telling people they weren't alone, and the loneliness you're carrying now. Those two truths in one person say a lot.

Eight months is still so fresh. You spent three decades with a structure, a role, a whole identity built into how you moved through the world. Of course you don't know who you are without it yet. That's not a failure — that's just where you are.

I'm hearing that you *were* someone who pointed people toward not being alone. So you understand, probably deeply, what it means to be unseen, even when you're surrounded by people.

Can I ask — when you say you don't know who you are now, what does that feel like from the inside? Is it more like everything is blank, or more like you're grieving who you were, or something else entirely?

**Seeker**: It feels like... both, actually. There's this blankness where the certainty used to be. I had answers. People came to me in crisis and I had something to offer them. Now someone asks me what I believe and I just — nothing.

But underneath that is something angrier. Because I'm grieving a version of myself that I'm not even sure was real. Was I genuinely faithful, or was I just very good at performing faith for thirty years? I don't know how to grieve something when you're not sure it ever existed.

**Minister**: That's a much harder thing to sit with than just the blankness — it's the blankness *plus* the question of whether the person who filled it was even real. That's not just loss. That's a kind of betrayal of yourself.

Thirty years is long enough that the performance and the faith probably got so tangled you can't separate them anymore. Maybe they were never meant to be separated — maybe the showing up, the speaking, the presence with people in crisis *was* part of your faith, even if you can't untangle where one ended and the other began. But I hear you: right now it all feels suspect. Like you can't trust your own thirty years.

Here's what I'm noticing, though — and tell me if this lands or if I'm off. You're grieving *two* things at once, and they're pulling in different directions. One is "I don't have answers anymore," and the other is "I'm not sure I ever had them to begin with." Those need different things, maybe.

Can I ask you this, and I'm genuinely curious: In those thirty years, when you sat with someone in actual crisis — not in a performance moment, but in a real one — what did you *actually* offer them? Not what you were supposed to offer. What did they seem to need from you, and what did you give?

**Seeker**: That question cuts.

The honest answer is — I don't think most of them needed the theology. They needed someone to stay in the room. To not flinch. I got good at that. Sitting with a woman at 2am after her husband hit her again, or with parents when the doctor said there was nothing more to do. I stayed. I didn't leave.

I'm not sure how much God had to do with that. That might have just been... me.

And that's part of what's so disorienting now. Because if the real thing I was offering was just human presence, then what was the thirty years of doctrine for? What was the institution for? I watched that same woman go back to her husband because the church told her marriage was sacred. I stayed in the room and then I handed her back to the system that was killing her.

That's not something I can make peace with. I don't care how elegant the theodicy is.

**Minister**: You just named something that most people spend a lifetime avoiding: the gap between what you were taught to believe and what you actually *saw* happen to the people you loved. And you're not abstracting it — you're holding a specific woman's face, a specific night at 2am. That's not theology. That's moral clarity, and it's agonizing.

Here's what I'm hearing underneath: You're not actually angry that you performed faith. You're angry that you *believed* the system — that you let yourself believe the doctrine was big enough to hold what you were witnessing, and it wasn't. And then you stayed in the room anyway, which made you complicit in handing people back to something that was hurting them. That's a real wound, not a spiritual crisis you can think your way out of.

The blankness now — the "nothing" when someone asks what you believe — that might not be a loss of faith. It might be the first honest thing you've said in thirty years.

But I want to ask you something, and I want you to really sit with it: When you stayed in that room with that woman at 2am — when you didn't flinch, when you bore witness to her suffering — *what did you believe about her in that moment?* Not about marriage. Not about God's plan. About *her*. What was true about her that made you stay?
