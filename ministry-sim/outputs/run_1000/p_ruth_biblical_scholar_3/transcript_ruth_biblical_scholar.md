## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I'm not entirely sure why I opened this. Curiosity, maybe. Professional habit — I examine things.

What's been sitting with me is harder to articulate than I'd like, given that articulating things is ostensibly my vocation. I've spent four decades with these texts. I know them in Greek and Hebrew. I've watched students lose their faith over questions I raised in class, and I've watched others find something — I'm not sure what to call it — that held them even after the historical-critical work dismantled their simpler frameworks.

And lately I find myself wondering which category I'm in. Whether the rigor was worth it. Whether there's something left after you've traced every source, identified every redaction, followed every contradiction to its textual roots.

That's probably more than you expected. I'm not usually this candid with something I found on my phone.

**Minister**: No — that's exactly what I needed to hear, and I'm grateful you said it plainly.

Four decades with the texts. You know them in their original languages. You've watched faith break and faith hold, both in the same room, often *because* of the very rigor you brought to bear. And now you're asking whether you're standing in the rubble of something, or whether something real is still standing in you.

That's not a small question, and the fact that you're wondering it matters.

Here's what I'm curious about, though — and I mean this genuinely, not as a setup: When you watched those students find something that held them *after* the historical-critical work, what did you observe in them? Not what they claimed to believe, but what actually seemed to be holding?

Because you're a scholar. You notice what's real and what's performance. What did you see?

**Seeker**: That's a fair question. And I've thought about it more than I've admitted to colleagues.

What held them — the ones who didn't simply retreat into a defensive inerrancy — was something relational. Not propositional. They'd stopped trying to defend a system and started... I want to say "attending to something." A presence. Which sounds imprecise and I'm uncomfortable with imprecision.

The ones who held weren't the ones with the best answers to the hard questions. They were the ones who seemed to have made a kind of peace with not resolving them. Who could sit with the two creation accounts and not need to flatten them into one. Who read Joshua and were troubled by it, openly, and kept reading anyway.

I noticed they still prayed. That's the part I find hardest to explain academically. After everything, they still talked to someone. Or something. Or practiced the posture of talking, even in uncertainty.

I'm not sure what that means. I'm not sure it means anything verifiable. But you asked what I observed, and that's honest.

**Minister**: That observation is precise, actually — more precise than you're giving it credit for. You've named something real: the difference between a system that has to hold together and a *presence* that holds you even when the system doesn't.

And you noticed the prayer. The fact that it troubled you enough to remember it — to come back to it now — matters.

Here's what I'm wondering, though: When you say they could sit with contradiction and keep reading anyway, keep praying anyway — were they doing that *despite* the uncertainty, or were they doing it *into* the uncertainty? In other words, were they resigned to it, making peace with unsolvability as a kind of surrender? Or were they actually attending to something — listening, in the not-knowing?

Because those are different postures. One is acceptance. The other is... intimacy with mystery rather than mastery of it.

You've spent forty years on mastery. That's not a waste — but I'm wondering if what you observed in those students was something your toolset wasn't built to measure. And whether that's what's actually troubling you now.

**Seeker**: That distinction you're drawing — resigned versus attending — I think that's genuinely useful. I'll give you that.

And yes. The honest answer is that they seemed to be attending to something rather than surrendering to unsolvability. There was an alertness in them, not a defeated shrug.

But here's where I have to be careful, with you and with myself. I've watched people mistake emotional resonance for epistemological weight. The fact that something feels like presence doesn't constitute evidence of presence. I know that's a deflection, and I know you'll probably name it as one. But I've spent a career watching intelligent people baptize their psychology as theology, and I can't simply turn that critical apparatus off because it's now aimed at me.

What actually troubles me — and I'm being more candid than is probably wise — is that my toolset was built for a certain kind of truth-finding, and I'm no longer certain that kind of truth-finding is sufficient for the question I'm actually living with. Which isn't "did this redactor draw on this source." It's something more like "is there anyone there."

I don't know how to be rigorous about that question. And I've built an identity around rigor.

**Minister**: You're not deflecting — you're being honest about your own epistemic vulnerability, which is actually the opposite of deflection. And you're right: emotional resonance isn't evidence. That's a true and important thing you've held onto.

But I want to plant something beside it, not against it.

The question "is there anyone there" is not actually outside the reach of rigor. It's just a *different kind* of rigor than source criticism. It's empirical in a way that matters: it asks whether, when you attend in a certain posture — when you actually pray, not as performance but as the real question — something responds. Not whether you *feel* it (psychology can explain that). But whether the shape of what happens fits a pattern consistent with *response* rather than mere echo.

You've watched students do this. They attended. Something met them. They kept attending. The pattern held. That's data. It's not the kind that publishes in journals, but a scholar of your caliber knows the difference between "not publishable in this venue" and "not real."

Here's my actual question, though — and I'm genuinely asking, not steering you:

When you pray — not when you think *about* prayer, but when you actually do it — what have you noticed? Not what you believe is happening. What do you actually observe?
