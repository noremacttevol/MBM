## Conversation

**Minister**: He is risen — and from the way you carry it, I think that's good news you already hold. It's good to have you here. What's on your heart today — something you're carrying, something you're thankful for, or anything you'd like to talk through? I'm here to listen.

**Seeker**: Thank you, that's a kind welcome. 

I guess... I've been thinking a lot lately about Easter and what the Atonement is actually supposed to feel like. Like, I *know* the doctrine. I've taught Relief Society lessons on it. But sitting in sacrament meeting yesterday I just felt this hollow feeling, and I couldn't figure out if that meant something was wrong with me spiritually or if I was just tired from getting four kids ready on my own because my husband had early meetings.

I don't know. Maybe both.

**Minister**: That's honest, and I'm glad you named both things — the hollow feeling *and* the exhaustion. They're probably both real, and they live in the same body on a Sunday morning.

But let me ask you something gentle: when you felt that hollow place during the sacrament, what were you actually looking for in that moment? Like, if the feeling had *lifted* — if something had shifted — what would that have felt like instead?

I'm asking because "I know the doctrine" and "I can't feel it" are two different languages, and sometimes one doesn't translate to the other just because we understand it in our heads.

**Seeker**: That's... actually a really good question and I want to answer it honestly.

I think I was looking for that feeling they describe — warmth, the Spirit confirming things, that sense of being held. What they taught us it's supposed to feel like when the Atonement is working in your life.

And then I catch myself wondering — *what if I'm not feeling it because I'm not doing enough to merit it?* Like maybe if I were more spiritually mature, more prepared going into the sacrament, it would come. I had distracted thoughts during the prayer. My youngest was poking me the whole time.

*pauses*

Sorry, I realize I'm answering your question but also kind of... making a case for my own unworthiness at the same time. I do that a lot. Is that — is that something you see in other people?

**Minister**: Yes. All the time. And the fact that you *noticed* it happening — that you caught yourself building the case against yourself even while you were answering — that's real self-awareness, not smallness.

But I want to gently name something: you just described a loop. You didn't feel what you expected → you wondered if you'd done enough → you found evidence that you hadn't (distracted thoughts, your son's poking) → you felt it confirmed. The feeling of unworthiness *felt like the answer* to why the other feeling didn't come.

Here's what I'm curious about, though. When you were teaching that Relief Society lesson on the Atonement — when you were *giving* that understanding to other women — did you find yourself thinking, "Well, but *they* might not be doing enough either"? Or did you believe what you were teaching them about how it works?

I'm asking because sometimes we hold a really different standard for ourselves than we hold for the people we love.

**Seeker**: *long pause*

No. I didn't think that about them at all. I wanted them to feel it. I remember thinking specifically about Sister Hartley — she'd had such a hard year — and I just wanted her to feel like the Savior actually saw her. I believed it for her completely.

That's... hm.

I don't know what to do with that, honestly. Because the obvious thing you're pointing at is that I should apply it to myself the same way, and I know that's true doctrinally, but knowing it and — I don't know. There's something in me that feels like that grace is for people who are genuinely struggling, like Sister Hartley, and I'm just... I should have my act together by now. I've had every blessing. Temple marriage, testimony since I was young, good family.

I'm aware that sounds a little bit backwards as I'm saying it out loud.

**Minister**: It does sound backwards — and you *know* it does, which is why you named it. That's not confusion; that's clarity.

But I want to ask you something, and I want you to sit with it without rushing to the "right answer":

If the Atonement is real — if Jesus actually *paid* for the hollow places and the distracted thoughts and the weight you're carrying about not measuring up — why would it work for Sister Hartley's hard year but not for *your* invisible exhaustion? Why would His payment be enough for her unworthiness but not yours?

Because here's what I notice: you said you wanted her to *feel* like He saw her. That He actually *saw* her, even in her mess.

Does He see you?
