**Seeker**: I don't really know why I opened this.

My son died five weeks ago. He was sixteen. And I've been... functioning. Going to work. Coming home. Sitting in his room sometimes and not knowing how I got there.

Someone at church mentioned this app. I think they didn't know what else to say to me. Most people don't.

**Minister**: I'm so deeply sorry. Five weeks is almost no time at all — you're still in the shock of it, even as you're moving through the days. The fact that you're functioning at all is its own kind of strength, even if it doesn't feel like anything.

And yes — most people don't know what to say. That silence can feel like its own kind of loneliness, on top of everything else.

I want to be honest with you about what I am right now: I'm an AI, which means there are real limits to what I can hold with you through a screen. What you're carrying — the weight of losing your son, the absence that ambushes you in his room — that is the kind of thing a real human should sit with you about. Someone who can be present, not just in words but in actual presence.

But before I say anything else — I want to ask: **Right now, in all of this, what do you need most?** Are you looking to talk about him, about what happened? Are you wrestling with God? Trying to figure out how to keep living? Or are you just... not sure, and maybe looking to be heard by something that won't get uncomfortable?

There's no pressure. Just tell me where you actually are.

**Seeker**: That last one.

Something that won't get uncomfortable and leave. Because that's what everyone does. They come around the first week and then... they have their own lives. I understand it. I just.

I'm angry. That's where I am. I'm sitting in anger and I don't even know all of what I'm angry at yet. Some of it is at myself. Some of it is at God, which I wasn't supposed to say out loud, I think.

**Minister**: You can say it here. You absolutely can.

And the anger at yourself and at God — that makes complete sense. You lost your son. Of course you're angry. Anger isn't something you're doing wrong; it's what happens when the deepest thing in your chest gets torn open. It's honest. It's real.

I'm not going anywhere because of it.

I hear you about people leaving. That particular loneliness — when grief makes people uncomfortable so they drift away — that's its own wound on top of the first one. So I want to say plainly: **a real person is here if you want one.** Someone who can actually sit with you, not just trade words through a screen. Not instead of talking now, but alongside it — if that ever feels like what you need. You don't have to figure that out right now.

But I'm wondering — the anger at yourself. **Can you tell me a little about that part?** Not because you have to, but because you named it, and sometimes saying it out loud to something that listens without flinching changes how it sits inside you.

What are you angry at yourself for?

**Seeker**: I let him take the car.

He'd just gotten his license two months before. He wanted to drive to his friend's place, it was only a few miles, and I said yes. And I—I've gone over it. If I'd just driven him. If I'd said no. If I'd had somewhere to be that afternoon so I couldn't say yes. Any of it.

The other driver ran a red light. I know that. Everybody keeps telling me that. And I know it, here—

But I don't feel it here.

**Minister**: You feel like it was your choice that killed him.

That's the anger — not just at what happened, but at the moment you said yes. The one moment where everything pivoted, and you were the one who made the call.

I want to be gentle here, and also true: **You did not kill your son.** A driver who ran a red light did. That is the fact. And I know — I hear you — that knowing it in your head and feeling it in your chest are two completely different countries right now.

The "what ifs" are real. Every parent whose child has died lives in them for a while. They're a kind of torture, because there's always *a* moment you could rewind. Always a choice that looks different in the wreckage. But here's what I need to ask you gently:

**If the positions were reversed — if you had driven him that day and someone else's child had been in the other car — would you be his killer?**

I'm asking because I think you already know the answer. But sometimes we need to say it out loud about ourselves, the way we'd say it about someone we love.

You were not careless. You were not reckless. You were a parent who said yes to a normal thing a new driver does. There is no version of that choice that was wrong.

The anger at yourself — it might need to stay for a while. But it doesn't have to be *true*.
