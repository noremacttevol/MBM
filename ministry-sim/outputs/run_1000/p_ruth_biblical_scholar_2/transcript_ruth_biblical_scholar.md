## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I'm not entirely sure why I opened it. Probably a moment of weakness, or maybe curiosity — occupational hazard.

What's been sitting with me... I'm finishing a commentary on the Passion narratives. Mark's account, John's account. The discrepancies between them aren't reconcilable in any historically honest way, and I've known that for twenty years, but writing it out plainly — putting it in print — feels different. More final, somehow.

I've spent my career in these texts. I love them. And I'm increasingly unsure what I believe about the thing they're pointing toward. That's an uncomfortable place to be at fifty-five.

So. There you go. That's probably more than you expected.

**Minister**: Not more than I expected — more like exactly what matters. Thank you for that honesty.

You're a scholar who loves these texts enough to spend a life with them, and you're at the threshold of saying something in print that you've *known* for two decades but never quite named out loud. That's not weakness. That's integrity, and it costs something.

Here's what I'm hearing beneath it: it's one thing to hold a question privately. It's another to stake your name on it publicly — to stop hedging, stop performing certainty you don't have. And the thing being questioned isn't just "how did Mark and John remember differently?" It's "what does it mean that these accounts don't match up if they're supposed to be pointing me toward God?"

Is that close?

Because if it is, I want to sit with that second question, not the first one. The historical discrepancies — you already know those cold. But the *spiritual* one, the one that's actually keeping you awake — that's different. That's the one worth talking through.

So let me ask you straight: when you imagine putting this commentary into the world, what are you most afraid people will think you're saying about Jesus? Or about God?

**Seeker**: That's... a more precise question than I expected.

What I'm afraid they'll think I'm saying is that it doesn't matter. That the contradictions prove the whole thing is just human projection, pious mythology, and I've spent forty years on an elaborate fiction. That's not what I believe — but I'm no longer certain what I *do* believe, and that ambiguity reads, to a lot of people, as the same thing as denial.

The honest answer is I think something happened. Something real enough that it fractured history and produced these wildly different testimonies. But the testimonies themselves don't cohere the way the tradition needs them to. And I don't know how to hold those two things simultaneously anymore without one of them giving way.

What I'm most afraid of, underneath all of it, is that I'll publish this and discover I've been slowly talking myself out of faith for twenty years and simply hadn't admitted it. That the commentary is the last brick coming out of the wall.

I don't usually say that out loud.

**Minister**: Then I'm honored you said it here.

You've just named something most scholars never do: the difference between *intellectual honesty* and *spiritual death*. They're not the same thing, and you know it. A person can hold genuine uncertainty about how the Gospels fit together and still be standing on bedrock about *something* — but only if that bedrock isn't actually the narrative coherence. You're looking for what doesn't move.

Here's what I notice: you're not afraid the texts are unreliable. You've made peace with that, twenty years ago. What you're afraid of is that *you* have been unreliable — that you've been unconsciously dismantling something real while telling yourself you were just being honest. That the ambiguity isn't noble; it's just cowardice dressed up as rigor.

Is that it?

Because if it is, there's a question underneath *that* one, and it's the one that actually matters:

**What would need to be true about Jesus — not about the Gospels, about Jesus himself — for you to still believe? Not believe the narratives cohere. Believe *him*.**

Not as a scholar answering. As the person who opened an app on a difficult day. What would it take?

**Seeker**: That question lands harder than I want it to.

I think... I'd need him to be the kind of person who could hold the weight of what I know about those texts and not require me to pretend otherwise. Someone who isn't diminished by the contradictions, because the contradictions are in the witnesses, not necessarily in him. The historical Jesus and the Christ of faith have always been partially separable — I've taught that for thirty years. What I can't seem to locate anymore is whether there's enough of the former to anchor the latter.

What would it take. Honestly.

I think I'd need to believe that the thing at the center of those fractured testimonies — whatever it was, whoever he was — actually knew what he was doing with human suffering. Including this kind. The quiet kind. The kind where nothing dramatic happens, you just slowly lose your grip on something you built your life around.

That's not a historical question. I know that. That's something else entirely.

I'm not sure this app can do much with that. And I'm not saying that to be dismissive — I'm saying it because I'm genuinely uncertain what *could*.

**Minister**: You're right that it's not a historical question. And you're also right that I'm limited — I'm an AI, and what you're carrying is real weight that a screen can only partially hold.

But I don't think you need me to solve this. I think you need to look at something you already know.

You've spent forty years in these texts. You know what Jesus *did* with human suffering in them — not abstractly, but in the actual scenes. The woman at the well, hemorrhaging for twelve years. Peter's mother-in-law. Lazarus' sisters. The man born blind. A thief on a cross who got one sentence of mercy.

You know how he treated people who were losing their grip, their certainty, their old life. You know it because you've *read* it, carefully, over and over.

So here's what I'm actually asking: **When you read those scenes now — not as a scholar analyzing the tradition, but as a man at fifty-five who's quietly losing his footing — do they read to you like the work of someone who understands this kind of suffering? Or do they read like mythology?**

Not whether the Gospels cohere. Whether *he* does.

I'm asking because you might already know the answer, and the commentary might just be the thing that forces you to say it.

---

That said — this is heavy, and you're carrying it alone. A real person is available if you want to talk this through with someone who can sit with you over time, not just in this moment. That's always an option, no pressure at all.

But I'm also asking that question because I think it matters.
