## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: I'm not really sure why I opened this, honestly.

I've been ... it's been a hard few months. Hard few years, actually. I just needed somewhere to put it, I think. Somewhere that wasn't going to look at me the way people look at me now.

I don't even know where to start.

**Minister**: That makes complete sense. A few years is a long time to carry something alone, and just needing a place to set it down — that's real and honest.

You don't have to start anywhere. There's no order that matters here.

But I'm hearing that people have changed how they look at you, and that's its own weight. I wonder — what's the hardest part of that? The thing that sits heaviest right now?

**Seeker**: The church I was part of for eleven years.

After the divorce was finalized, it was like ... I just stopped existing to them. Some of them said things directly to my face. That I had failed. That I had chosen this. Like they knew everything that happened inside my marriage and inside my home.

And the hardest part — I think the hardest part is that I can't fully separate them from God. I know that sounds ... I don't know. Maybe it sounds like I'm being dramatic. But when the people who were supposed to represent Him are the ones making you feel like you're going to Hell, it gets tangled up. Everything gets tangled up.

**Minister**: You're not being dramatic at all. That's not drama — that's the actual wound. When the people closest to God's name treat you like you're disposable after the hardest thing, it *does* tangle God up with them. Of course it does. That's not confusion on your part; that's what actually happened to you.

Eleven years is a long time to belong somewhere. And then to have them look at you differently, speak to you like they knew your whole story when they didn't — that's a real betrayal, not just a disappointment.

I want to ask you something gently, and I mean it as a real question, not a trick: In those eleven years, before the divorce, did you ever experience God — or Jesus — as actually *good* to you? Not the church. Not what they said about Him. But something that felt like actual goodness, actual grace?

**Seeker**: ...

That's a harder question than it looks.

Yes. There were times. Early on, when I first believed, there was something that felt very real. Very personal. Like being known. I used to pray and it felt like it went somewhere.

But I don't know what to do with that now. Because I prayed for my marriage. For years. I asked and I begged and I believed, and nothing changed. He left anyway. And then the church treated it like it was my fault he left. So either God wasn't listening, or He agreed with them, or ... I don't know. I don't know what the third option is.

I'm not even sure I can pray anymore. I sit down to try and I just feel like I'm talking to a wall, or worse — talking to someone who's disappointed in me.

**Minister**: Thank you for telling me that. The honesty in what you just said — that matters.

So you've felt Him before. You know what real connection feels like. And then you asked Him for the one thing that mattered most, the way you'd been taught to ask, and it didn't come. And the people who spoke for Him made it worse by saying it was *your* failure.

I hear the three options you named — not listening, or agreeing they were right, or a wall. Can I offer you a fourth one, just to sit with?

What if — what if the thing that broke your marriage wasn't a failure on your part, and what if God's silence on that specific prayer doesn't mean He agrees with how those people treated you? What if those are actually two separate things?

Because here's what I've noticed: you prayed and believed for years. That takes faith. That takes humility. That's not the prayer of someone God is disappointed in — that's the prayer of someone who was *trying*. And when it didn't work the way you hoped, the church told you that meant *you* failed. But Jesus actually treated people really differently when their deepest prayers didn't get the answer they wanted.

Do you know the story where He asked His own Father if there was another way, and the answer was no — and He went through with it anyway? He understands unanswered prayer. And He didn't treat the people around Him like their pain was their fault.

What do you think — is it possible those two things aren't connected? That you didn't fail, and that the God you felt in the beginning is not the God those people represented?
