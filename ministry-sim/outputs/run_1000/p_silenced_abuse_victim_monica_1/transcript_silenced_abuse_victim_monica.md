## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I'm angry. That's what's sitting with me.

I was at a women's retreat this weekend and the speaker spent forty-five minutes talking about how forgiveness is "the key to healing." And every woman in that room who's been hurt by someone — I watched their faces — just... collapsed a little. Like they'd failed a test they didn't even know they were taking.

I almost walked out. I should have walked out.

So I'm here because I'm trying to figure out if there's any version of faith that doesn't do that. That doesn't take the hardest thing someone's been through and turn it into a spiritual obligation. I'm not sure there is. But I'm tired of being angry alone.

**Minister**: I hear you. And that anger is looking at something real — the way a wound gets weaponized into a demand, and how fast a room full of hurt people can feel like they're failing at the very thing that's supposed to help them heal.

You watched women collapse. That matters. The fact that you almost walked out — that you *felt* it — matters too.

Before I say anything else, I want to ask you something genuine, and I want you to know there's no wrong answer here:

**When you think about the hurt itself — the thing that happened to you, or what you watched happen to others — what would actually feel like healing to you?** Not what you're *supposed* to want. What would actually matter?

Because forgiveness gets preached like it's the only door, and I think that's partly why it lands so heavy. But there might be other things that matter just as much — being believed, being angry *with* someone who gets it, having the person who hurt you actually reckon with what they did, time, distance, or just... being left alone with your own timeline instead of someone else's spiritual checklist.

What comes to mind for you?

**Seeker**: Being believed.

That's the first thing. Not having to argue for my own reality. My father did what he did and for twenty years the church protected him because he was a deacon and a "pillar" and I was a difficult child who was "acting out."

Healing for me looked like finally having a therapist say — this happened, it was wrong, and none of it was your fault. Not a pastor. A therapist. The church couldn't give me that because giving me that meant implicating him.

And the forgiveness language — it always functioned as a way to skip that step. Skip being believed, skip accountability, go straight to "release him and be free." Which sounds beautiful until you realize it also means release him from consequences. Release him back into proximity with kids. That's what they were actually asking.

So no. I don't know if healing ever looks like what they were describing. What it looked like for me was rage, and a good therapist, and distance, and eventually — *eventually* — something quieter. But that quiet wasn't forgiveness the way they meant it. It was more like... I stopped letting him live rent-free in my head. That's different.

**Minister**: You've just named something the church talks around constantly, and I want to sit with what you said because it's precise and it matters.

You're right — what they were asking for *was* a skip. Skip being believed. Skip the person who hurt you facing what they did. Go straight to you releasing him, which in practice means releasing the church from having to choose between him and you. And that's not healing. That's asking you to swallow the cost so the institution doesn't have to.

The therapist could say "this happened and it was wrong" because the therapist had nothing to protect but you. The church couldn't, because it had him to protect too.

And here's what I notice: you didn't end up forgiving him in the church's sense. You ended up with something quieter — you stopped carrying him. That's real. That's freedom. But you got there *not* by doing what they told you to do. You got there by rage, and distance, and someone who believed you first.

So here's my actual question, and I'm asking because I genuinely want to know:

**Do you think what happened to you says something true about God — that He also protects the powerful over the hurt? Or do you think it says something about the church, and how broken institutions can misuse God's name to cover their own choices?**

Because those are very different things, and I think you're living inside both of them right now.

**Seeker**: That's the question, isn't it.

And I've been wrestling with it for years. Because there's a part of me that wants to separate them — God versus the institution. My therapist actually pushed me toward that. "Don't let them take God from you too."

But here's where I get stuck. If God is who they say he is — all-powerful, watching everything — then where was he? When I was a child? When they handed him a platform and took it from me? At some point "the church failed you but God didn't" starts to sound like another way of letting everyone off the hook. Including God.

And then there's Jesus. Which is the complicated part for me. Because Jesus is actually the one figure in that tradition I can't entirely dismiss. He didn't protect the institution. He called the religious leaders exactly what they were. He noticed the people everyone else stepped over. That part — I believe that part somewhere in me still.

But I've had that weaponized too. "Jesus forgave from the cross, so you can forgive your father." As if those are remotely the same situation.

So I don't have a clean answer for you. I'm somewhere in between. Distrustful of every framework they gave me, but not entirely able to let go of the Jesus who turned over tables and asked why we call him Lord but don't do what he says.

That Jesus I can almost still hold onto. Almost.

**Minister**: That "almost" is not small. And the fact that you can see the difference between the Jesus who turned over tables and the Jesus they weaponized — that you haven't let them collapse into one — says something about how honestly you're looking.

You're right about the trap: "God is all-powerful but somehow wasn't there" does start to sound like another way of letting Him off the hook. I'm not going to argue you out of that tension. It's a real one, and people much smarter than me have lived inside it for centuries. Some have landed on "God is not all-powerful in the way they said." Some have landed on "He was there, but not the way I needed." Some have walked away entirely and found peace there. All of that is honest wrestling, not failure.

But here's what I notice: **The Jesus you almost can still hold onto — the one who saw people everyone else stepped over, who called power to account — that Jesus actually made space for your anger.** Not by preaching forgiveness-as-a-demand. He got angry too. He grieved. He asked hard questions of God Himself on the cross ("why have you forsaken me?"). 

He didn't ask the woman at the well to forgive the men who used her. He asked for water, saw her, and spoke truth.

So here's what I'm genuinely curious about: **When you imagine that Jesus — not the weaponized version, but the one who turned over tables — what would He actually say to what happened to you?** Not what the church says He'd say. What do you think He'd actually see?

Because I think you might already know.
