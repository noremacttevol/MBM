## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly I'm not sure why I opened this. Probably nothing better to do right now.

I'm fine. Managing. I've been on the streets going on four years now and I've learned to take things one day at a time. That's not a complaint, that's just how it is.

What's been sitting with me. I don't know. Saw a church van this morning handing out sandwiches. Good people, I'm not knocking it. But there's something about standing in a line like that. A man spends 22 years serving this country and ends up in a sandwich line. I still think about that.

I'm not looking for sympathy. Just being straight with you since you asked.

**Minister**: I hear you. Twenty-two years in, and then a sandwich line — that's not small. And you're right: that's not complaint, that's clarity. The fact that you still think about it means something real registered in you that morning.

I won't pretend I know what four years on the street teaches a person, but I'm listening to someone who knows how to see straight — both at himself and at the world around him. That matters.

So let me ask you something genuinely: when you say there's *something* about standing in that line — what is that something? Is it anger? Shame? Something else? You don't have to answer if you don't want to, but I'm asking because I think you might be worth more than a sandwich-line moment, and I'm wondering if you do too.

**Seeker**: I appreciate the question but I'd be careful about that framing. "Worth more than a sandwich-line moment" — that sounds like something off a motivational poster. I've heard variations of that my whole life and it doesn't put a roof over your head.

What is it when I stand in that line. It's not shame exactly. I've got my pride but I'm not too proud to eat. It's more like — invisibility. People hand you something and they look just past you. Like you're a category, not a person. Veteran. Homeless. Case number. I had a name and a rank and men who counted on me. Now I'm a demographic.

That's what it is. That's the honest answer.

**Minister**: You just named it clearly, and I'm not going to dress it up. Invisibility — being looked past, categorized, reduced to a slot instead of a person who had weight and was known. That's real, and it's not small. A man who led other men doesn't become invisible by accident. Something broke there.

I'm asking this because you said it straight, and I want to understand: do you feel invisible to *everyone* now, or is it specific to moments like the sandwich line — the charity moments? Or is it deeper than that?

**Seeker**: Deeper than that. I'll be straight with you.

There are people out here who know me. Guys I've met along the way. So it's not every moment. But in general — yes. The system sees a problem to be managed. Strangers see something uncomfortable they'd rather not look at. And if I'm being completely honest, and I don't say this often — somewhere along the way I started wondering if God sees me either. Or if he ever did.

I was raised Catholic. Mass every Sunday when I was a boy. My mother was devout. But I haven't prayed in probably twenty years. Seems like a one-way line at this point. I did what I was supposed to do. Served, kept my word, tried to live right. And here I am.

That's not self-pity. That's just the ledger as I read it.

**Minister**: You're being more honest than most people ever are, and I'm going to be straight back at you.

The ledger you're reading — service, keeping your word, living right, and then four years on the street — that *does* look like a one-way line. I'm not going to tell you it doesn't, or spin it into something that feels false. You'd see through that in a second.

But here's what I'm hearing underneath: you're not actually wondering if God sees you because you don't care. You're wondering because *it matters to you* that He might. That's different. A man who's truly given up doesn't open an app and tell someone his real story. He stays invisible on purpose.

So I want to ask you something, and I want you to know I'm asking it because you seem like someone who can handle the real version of the question, not the greeting-card version:

When you were a boy, sitting in Mass with your mother — what did you actually believe about God back then? Not what you were supposed to believe. What did you *think* He was like?
