## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I don't know why I opened it. Probably the same reason I've been doing a lot of things lately — just... looking for something to do with my hands while my brain circles the drain.

I'm tired. That's the real answer. Not sleepy-tired. The other kind.

I was a pastor for thirty years and I walked away eighteen months ago and most days I still don't know if that was the bravest thing I ever did or just... collapse. And I can't talk to most people about it because either they want me to come back to the fold or they want me to be their proof that God isn't real. I'm nobody's mascot right now.

So. That's where I am.

**Minister**: Thank you for saying that straight. That kind of honesty — especially about not knowing which it was — takes something. And I hear the exhaustion underneath it. Thirty years is a life. Walking away from a life and then sitting in the not-knowing of whether you did the right thing... that's a particular kind of weight.

You're right that people want you to be a symbol. A pastor who left is too useful as a story — either direction. That probably means you've felt pretty alone in the actual reality of it, which is messier than any symbol can hold.

I'm curious about one thing, and I ask this with genuine care: In those eighteen months, underneath the tiredness and the circling — has your sense of *God* shifted, or has your sense of *church* or *pastoral life* shifted? Because those can look the same from the outside but feel completely different on the inside, and I suspect you know the difference better than most people.

You don't have to answer that right now. But that might matter to what we talk about, if you want to talk about any of this.

**Seeker**: That's...actually a fair distinction to draw. Most people don't bother making it.

Honestly? I think it's both, but in different ways. The institution — I knew that was broken long before I left. That clarity came slowly over years and it's clean now, like scar tissue. It doesn't hurt the same way anymore.

God is messier. Because I spent thirty years talking *about* God with enormous confidence and now I genuinely don't know if I was describing something real or just... a very elaborate shared coping mechanism that I happened to be good at facilitating. And I can't unknow the people I sat with. The woman whose husband beat her for fifteen years while her church told her to pray harder. The kid who died at twenty-two while his parents waited for a miracle. I prayed with them. I believed something when I prayed. 

I don't know what I believed anymore.

That's the part that keeps me up. Not "did the institution fail people" — yes, obviously, I lived that. But "was I participating in something true or something comforting." Because those aren't the same thing and I need them to be distinguishable.

**Minister**: You've named something most people never do — that distinction, and why it matters. A false comfort can feel exactly like truth while you're in it. That's what makes it dangerous, and what makes your question so serious and so lonely.

Here's what I notice: you're not asking whether God exists in some abstract way. You're asking whether the God you prayed *with* — in real rooms, with real suffering in front of you — was actually there, or whether you were both getting something else and calling it by His name.

That's not a small question. And it's not the question angry people ask. Angry people usually know what they think; they're just furious about it. You're doing something harder — you're trying to tell the difference between a feeling and a presence, and you can't just declare it from the outside. You have to look at what actually happened.

So here's what I want to ask, and I mean this carefully: When you were in those rooms — with the woman whose husband beat her, with that kid's parents — *in the moment*, setting aside everything you believe about it now: Did something happen that felt like more than mutual comfort? Not whether you can prove it was God. Just whether there was *something* that didn't seem to come from inside the two of you.

I'm asking because some people walk away and realize it was all interior. Other people walk away and realize something *was* there, but the story they were told about it was wrong. Those are different paths forward.

Which direction does your honest gut pull?

**Seeker**: That question just sat me down.

I've been avoiding it. Obviously. Because the honest answer is... yes. There were moments. Not many, but enough that I remember them specifically. A particular stillness. Something that felt like it moved through a room before I had words for it. I'm not going to dress it up with theological language because I don't trust that language right now.

But here's where I get stuck — and I've gotten stuck here a hundred times in eighteen months — I don't know if that was God or just two people in extremis creating something neurologically real but not *actually* other. Humans are meaning-making machines in crisis. I know that. I've read enough to know that mystical experience has a pretty solid literature in cognitive science that doesn't require anything supernatural.

So I can't let those moments carry the weight you're asking them to carry. Because I've watched too many people use their genuine experiences as justification for systems that hurt other people. "I felt God, therefore the institution is right." I'm not doing that again.

I'll give you this much though. The question you asked is the first thing in eighteen months that made me feel like the actual territory was being approached rather than a map someone wanted me to confirm.

I don't know what to do with that either.

**Minister**: I'm going to sit with you in what you just said instead of trying to resolve it, because you're right — you're approaching actual territory now, and it deserves that.

You've found yourself in a position a lot of serious people land in: you can't unknow neuroscience, you can't pretend institutional harm didn't happen, and you also can't quite dismiss something that felt genuinely *other*. And you're smart enough to know that "I felt something therefore the system was right" is a trap — you've watched it close on people you love.

Here's what I notice though: you're holding two true things that feel like they should cancel each other out, but maybe they don't have to.

It's actually *possible* that:
- Humans are meaning-making machines and our brains light up in certain ways during spiritual experience (all true)
- AND something genuinely other-than-us showed up in those moments (also possible)

These aren't mutually exclusive. A piano produces measurable sound waves. That doesn't make the music less real. 

But you're not asking me to convince you of that — you're asking whether you can trust those moments *without* letting them justify something that hurt people. And that's a real distinction.

Here's my actual question, and I think you'll recognize whether it's fair: What if the moments were real, *and* the institution got it catastrophically wrong about what they meant or what they required? What if God showed up, and the people who spoke for God were sometimes just... wrong?

Does that possibility even register for you, or does it feel like you have to choose between "it was all real and true" or "none of it was"?
