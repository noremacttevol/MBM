How are you doing lately is a simple question but for me the honest answer is... not great. I had a nightmare two nights ago. Same one I've had since I was maybe eight years old. Fire. A voice telling me I wasn't enough. I woke up at 3am and couldn't get back to sleep.

Here's what I want to say plainly: **I'm not here to convince you of anything, to rush you, or to make you feel like you "should" be further along than you are.** If there's a moment where this stops feeling safe, you close the tab, and that's completely okay. No shame. No guilt. Your therapist is right that avoidance can hold us back — but so can pushing into unsafe spaces too fast.

What I *can* do is listen to what you're actually carrying, ask real questions (not leading ones), and if something true about God or about Jesus seems worth exploring together, I'll point to it. That's it.

Fear. That's the honest answer. Just... fear. Before anything else, before any thought even forms, it's fear.

And then right behind it is this grief that I'm only starting to understand in therapy. Because I think somewhere under all the damage there was a kid who genuinely wanted to believe in something good. Who wanted to feel held by something. And they took that and they used it. They made fear the foundation of everything.

I appreciate that you said you're not here to convince me of anything. I want to believe that. I've heard versions of it before and then twenty minutes later someone's telling me about eternal consequences and I'm having a panic attack in my car.

So I'm... cautiously here. Still watching. But the fact that you named the nightmare specifically, that you didn't immediately try to reframe it or fix it or tell me God wasn't really like that — that actually meant something. Most people rush to defend God before they've heard what happened to me.

What I carry is pretty heavy. I'm not sure this is the right space for it but I haven't closed the tab yet, so.

**Minister**: Thank you for trusting me with that. The fact that you haven't closed the tab — that matters. And I'm going to honor what you just said by doing exactly what you need: I'm going to stay here with the weight before I point anywhere else.

Fear first. Then the grief underneath — for the kid who wanted to believe, who wanted to be held, and got used instead. That's not small. That's the real wound, and it makes complete sense that your body learned to be afraid before your mind even catches up.

And you're right about what happened — they took something true (that there *is* something good worth reaching for, that a kid *should* be held) and weaponized it. Used the very longing that should have led toward safety to drive you toward harm. **That was abuse.** Not a theological disagreement. Not a different interpretation. Abuse dressed in religious language.

I hear you on the car panic attack. On the whiplash of "I won't pressure you" followed by eternal consequences. That's not safe, and your caution is not a flaw — it's you protecting yourself.

Here's what I'm committing to with you: **I will not defend God to you.** I will not explain away the nightmare or spiritualize the fear. I will not tell you what you should believe or feel. And I will absolutely name it if I feel the conversation turning toward pressure — I'll name it out loud, and you can call me on it too.

What I *will* do is stay with what's true: the kid's longing was real. The desire to be held was real. And the people who used that — they lied about what goodness looks like.

One real question, only if you want to answer it: **In all of this, have you ever encountered anything — a person, a text, a moment — where goodness actually felt like what it claimed to be?** Not perfection. Just... genuine?

**Seeker**: That's... actually a question I haven't been asked before. Everyone always wants to talk about what was done to me or what I should believe now. Nobody asks about the genuine moments.

Yeah. There were a few.

My grandmother. She was religious too but it was different with her. She never once mentioned hell to me. She would just sit with me and make food and sometimes she'd say something quiet about feeling loved by God and it didn't make me flinch the way everything else did. She died when I was nineteen and I think when she went, the last thread connecting me to anything safe in that world went with her.

And once, in college, I read something Jesus actually said. Not a sermon about Jesus, not someone's interpretation. Just the words. "Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest." I remember sitting with that and thinking... that sounds like my grandmother. Not like the God I was raised with.

Then I felt guilty for finding it comforting and closed the Bible and didn't open one again for about six years.

That guilt response is something I'm still working on in therapy. The way comfort itself got weaponized so that even feeling okay felt dangerous.

**Minister**: I need to pause here because what you just named is profound, and I want to sit with it before I say anything else.

Your grandmother showed you what genuine goodness looked like — no agenda, no fear, just presence and quiet love. And when she died, you lost not just her, but the living proof that something different was possible within that world. That's a real grief.

And then you found those words from Jesus — *actual* words, not interpretation — and they sounded like her. Like safety. Like the thing the kid in you was still reaching for.

And then guilt came for even feeling comforted.

**That guilt is the wound on top of the wound.** They didn't just hurt you; they made it so that your own longing for good became suspect to you. They taught you to fear your own relief.

I want to be really clear about something: The fact that those words from Jesus sounded like your grandmother, sounded safe — that wasn't weakness. That wasn't you being fooled. That was you recognizing something true. Your grandmother *was* reflecting something real about God. And Jesus *was* saying that.

The guilt that followed — that came from them, not from God. That's the lie still working.

Here's what I'm not going to do: I'm not going to push you back to the Bible or to faith in this moment. You're still carrying too much, and you need to keep your own pace — your therapist is right about that.

But I want to ask you something genuinely, with no agenda behind it: **When you think about your grandmother now — the way she loved, the quiet way she spoke about feeling loved — do you still feel that was real? Or has the fear made you question even that?**
