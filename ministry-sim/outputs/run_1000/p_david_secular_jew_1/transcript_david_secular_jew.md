## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? My mother called last Sunday. She's 78, still lights Shabbat candles every week even though she lives alone now, and she asked me — again — when I'm going to "find my way back." 

I don't even know what that means for me. Back to what, exactly? I do Passover, I feel Jewish in my bones, but the synagogue stuff... it never quite took.

So I don't know. Maybe I opened this out of some vague guilt. Maybe curiosity. Probably both. I'm not looking to be saved, if that's where this is going — just being upfront about that.

**Minister**: Thank you for being that honest. And I hear something real in what you said — you carry both things at once: something deeply Jewish that lives in you (that's not small, and you're not wrong to honor it), *and* a genuine distance from the religious structures that were supposed to hold it. Your mom feels that gap, and now it's on your mind. That's not guilt you have to fix right this second — it's just... there.

I'm not here to save you, and I mean that. Not my job, not how this works. I'm just someone to think out loud with, if you want to.

When you say the synagogue stuff never quite took — was it the belief part that didn't land, or the practice, or something else? What was the actual friction?

**Seeker**: The belief part, mostly. I mean, I can do a Seder — I actually love the Seder, the argument, the wine, the whole chaotic production of it. That feels real. But sitting in shul on Yom Kippur asking forgiveness from a God I'm not sure is listening... it started feeling like I was performing sincerity, and I can't do that. I'm an accountant. I deal in what's real.

The God thing is where it falls apart for me. Either there's something there or there isn't, and I've never been able to convince myself there is — not with any intellectual honesty.

**Minister**: That's clear-eyed, and I respect it. You're not pretending; you won't fake it even to yourself. That matters.

So let me ask this genuinely — and I'm curious what your actual answer is, not what you think should be your answer: when you sit at that Seder, when you're in the argument and the wine and the whole thing *works* for you — what is it doing? What's real about it that *isn't* performing?

Because you said it feels real. Not that it's culturally important or that it honors your people (though it does), but that *it feels real*. I'm wondering what you mean by that.

**Seeker**: That's... actually a harder question than it sounds.

I think it's the continuity. My grandfather fled Poland with basically nothing, and he did that same Seder. His grandfather did it. There's something about sitting at that table and feeling like you're a link in a very long chain — that's not nothing. That's almost physical for me.

And the argument. Jews argue with the text, argue with each other, argue with God supposedly — there's something honest about a tradition that builds dissent into the ritual itself. That I can respect.

But look, I know what you might be doing here. You're going to suggest that longing for something real, something continuous, something bigger than me — that that's a God-shaped hole or whatever the phrase is. And I'd push back on that. I think it's just... human. It doesn't require a supernatural explanation.

**Minister**: You're right — I could go there, and you'd catch me doing it. So I won't.

But I'm genuinely not thinking what you think I'm thinking. I'm actually struck by something else.

You said the argument is what you respect — the tradition that *builds dissent into the ritual itself*. That a God worth anything wouldn't want fake sincerity; He'd want honest wrestling. That performing something you don't believe is worse than not showing up at all.

That's... actually pretty close to how the God that Jesus revealed operates. Not the distant, listening-or-not God you described sitting alone on Yom Kippur. But a God who *wanted* to be argued with. Jacob wrestled Him and got blessed for it. Job spent an entire book yelling at Him, and God's response was basically "good — at least you're honest." Jesus Himself asked God why He'd abandoned Him on a cross.

Not a God offended by your skepticism. A God who seems almost to prefer it to performance.

Does that land differently than what you expected me to say, or does it still feel like the same move dressed up prettier?
