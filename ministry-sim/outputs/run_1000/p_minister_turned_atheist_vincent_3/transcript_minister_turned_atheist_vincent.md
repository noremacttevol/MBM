I was a pastor for almost twenty years. I left the faith about four years ago — not because of a scandal, not because someone hurt me, but because I read too much and couldn't stop being honest with myself. Which sounds noble, but the actual experience of it has been... quieter than I expected. And lonelier.

So I'm here. Skeptical, but here.

**Minister**: That's a real thing you just said—the quiet loneliness of it. Not the dramatic kind, just the Sunday morning kind where the absence is clear. And the honesty about *how* you left, without a villain to blame—that takes a different kind of courage to live with.

Twenty years as a pastor means you weren't just attending something. You were *in* it, leading from it, probably counseling people through their deepest questions from what you believed. And then you couldn't anymore. That's not small.

I want to understand where you actually are, not assume. When you say you read too much and couldn't stop being honest—were you reading *against* faith, or reading Scripture and theology and finding it didn't hold up the way it used to? Or something else entirely?

**Seeker**: It wasn't reading against faith — that's an important distinction and I appreciate you asking it that way.

It was the opposite, actually. I wanted to go deeper. I started with serious Old Testament scholarship — Friedman, Finkelstein, the Documentary Hypothesis — because I thought understanding the text better would strengthen what I preached. And then I couldn't unknow what I'd learned. The contradictions weren't interpretive puzzles anymore, they were evidence of human authorship. Competing human voices. Political agendas. 

And then the New Testament. Historical Jesus scholarship. The gap between what Paul believed and what the Gospel of John claimed. The way the resurrection accounts don't actually harmonize if you read them carefully side by side.

I kept waiting for the faith to survive the scrutiny. It didn't.

What I find interesting about this app — and I mean this genuinely, not as an attack — is whether you're actually equipped to engage that, or whether you're going to eventually circle back to "but have you considered your heart might be closed." Because I've heard that one.

**Minister**: Fair question, and I respect the directness. Let me be equally direct: I *can* engage that—not because I have all the answers (I don't), but because hard questions about the text are not somehow off-limits for faith, and pretending they are is a failure. Retreating to "your heart is closed" when someone has done real scholarly work is indeed a way to avoid the actual argument, and you're right to call it out.

So I won't do that.

Here's what I will say honestly: The Documentary Hypothesis, the tensions in the Gospels, the historical-Jesus scholarship—those are real observations. Serious scholars who love Scripture grapple with them. Some stay believing. Some don't. The fact that you can name them doesn't prove anything either way about what's true.

But I'm curious about something, and I want to ask it without steering you: **When you read through the Gospels side by side and noticed the resurrection accounts don't harmonize perfectly—what did you do with the *content* of what they're describing?** Not the literary problem, but the thing itself. A dead man walking around. Appearing in a locked room. Being touched and eating fish. Something that broke every category the disciples had.

Because there's a difference between "these texts were written by different people with different theological concerns" (true) and "therefore the event they're all trying to describe didn't happen" (a separate conclusion). One is textual analysis. The other is a claim about history.

What pulled you from the first to the second?

**Seeker**: That's actually a fair distinction and not many people make it cleanly. I'll give you that.

But here's where I landed: when you have four accounts that can't agree on who went to the tomb, what they saw, what they were told, and what they did afterward — at some point the most parsimonious explanation isn't "eyewitnesses remembered differently." It's that these are theological constructions written decades later by communities with different needs.

And the content — the dead man walking, the locked room — I can't evaluate that claim in isolation from the reliability of the witnesses. That's not skepticism for its own sake. That's just how we assess any historical claim. Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, and what we have is contradictory late testimony filtered through theological agendas.

What moved me from textual analysis to historical conclusion was applying the same standards I'd apply to any other ancient claim. If someone told me Apollonius of Tyana raised the dead, I'd want better evidence than hagiographic accounts from his followers. I don't see why the method changes because the stakes feel higher to me personally.

What I genuinely couldn't resolve — and still can't — is why the standard of evidence should be different for something I want to be true.

**Minister**: Now that's the real argument, and it's honest. You're not dodging it with sentiment or retreating into "it just feels true." You're asking: *Why would I lower my epistemic standards for something I desperately want to believe?* That's the integrity question. And you lived it for twenty years as a pastor, which means you know what it costs to stop doing that.

I want to sit with this carefully, because there are a few moves I could make here that would be traps:

**I could argue that the Gospels are actually more reliable than you're saying.** I could push back on late dating, on the coherence of certain details, on whether contradictions about *who saw what* actually undermine the core claim. And there *are* serious scholars who do that work. But you already know that case exists, and you found it unconvincing. Making that argument again would just be me assuming you haven't thought carefully, which you clearly have.

**I could pivot to your heart—** suggest that something in you still *wants* this to be true, and that want is worth examining. But you just named that yourself. You *know* the wanting doesn't make it true. You've lived the cost of that knowledge.

So here's what I actually want to ask:

**When you apply the same historical standard to Apollonius or other miracle claims, are you doing that because the method is the most reliable way to know what happened—or because it's the method that produces the most skeptical conclusion?** 

In other words: if the Gospels had contradicted each other *less*, or if they'd been written earlier, would you believe the resurrection happened? Or would the bar keep moving?
