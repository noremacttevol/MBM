## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: My daughter died fourteen months ago. And the pastor at my church — the church I've attended for thirty-one years — stood up at her memorial and said she was lost. That's the word he used. *Lost.* Everyone in that room knew what he meant.

I don't know why I opened this. I'm still in that church. I don't know why I'm still in that church either.

**Minister**: I am so deeply sorry. Fourteen months is still so close — that wound is still open, and it should be.

And to hear that word spoken over your daughter in her own memorial, in front of everyone, when you are broken — I can feel the weight of that betrayal layered right on top of the grief itself. That's enormous. That's the kind of thing that makes you question everything you thought was solid.

You're still there. You show up. I'm not asking why yet — I just want you to know I see that you're carrying both things at once: the loss of her, and the loss of feeling safe in a place you've called home for thirty-one years.

Can I ask — and this is genuine, not leading anywhere — what your daughter was like? Not what anyone said about her soul or her status. Just... who was she to you?

**Seeker**: She was the smartest person I ever knew. Not just book smart — she could read a room, read people. She had this way of making everyone feel like they were the most interesting person she'd ever talked to.

She stopped believing when she was in college. We argued about it for years. But she never stopped being kind. She never stopped calling me every Sunday. She just... couldn't make herself believe something she didn't think was true. She said she tried.

And they said that's enough to damn her. That trying isn't enough. That not believing is the unforgivable thing.

I'm still angry. I want you to know that upfront. I'm not here looking for someone to talk me back into being okay with that.

**Minister**: I hear you. And I'm glad you said that upfront — your anger is not something to smooth over or pray away. It's the sound of a father who loved his daughter and is telling the truth about what happened to you both.

She sounds remarkable. The kind of person who actually *lived* what most people just talk about — she made people feel seen. She was honest, even when it cost her. And she called you every Sunday. That matters.

Here's what I want to say plainly: what that pastor said about your daughter — that she is lost, that her honest searching and her refusal to fake belief damns her — that is *not* what Jesus taught. I'm not softening that or hedging it. 

Jesus looked at the people around Him who *did* have all the right beliefs, who knew Scripture cold, and He was hardest on *them*. But the woman at the well, the thief on the cross, the Samaritan — people on the outside, people whose beliefs were all wrong by the standards of the day — He moved toward them with something that looked like recognition. Like He saw them.

Your daughter was honest. She was kind. She loved you. She refused to pretend.

I want to ask you something, and I mean it as a real question, not a setup: What do *you* know about what God is actually like? Not what you've been told — what do *you* believe, in your gut, about whether a God worth calling good would damn a person like that?

**Seeker**: She was my daughter, not my father. I want to be clear about that.

And I appreciate what you're trying to do with the Jesus angle. I know those stories. I grew up in them. But the same book that has the thief on the cross has John 14:6. "No one comes to the Father except through me." My pastor didn't make that up. It's in there.

What do I believe in my gut? I believe that if that doctrine is true — if the system is exactly what my church says it is — then the God running it is not good. I can't dress that up. And if He's not good, I don't know what I'm supposed to do with thirty-one years of my life.

That's what I actually believe right now. I'm not looking for you to fix it.

**Minister**: You're right. I apologize — your daughter. That matters.

And you're not asking me to fix anything. You're naming something true, and you're asking me to hear it without flinching. I will.

You know the text. You know the doctrine. And you've looked at it honestly and said: *if this is how it works, then the God at the center of it is cruel*. And you can't unknow that. You can't go back to thirty-one years of faith on the other side of seeing that clearly.

That's not stupid. That's not a crisis of faith that needs management. That's a collision between what you were taught about God and what you actually see when you look at your daughter — and at yourself, at what goodness actually looks like — and they don't match.

Here's the thing though: that collision is between *two versions of what Jesus taught*. Not between faith and reason. Your pastor reads John 14:6 one way. There are people — faithful, thoughtful people who love Scripture just as much — who read it differently. Not as a legal door that only opens one way, but as Jesus saying something about *relationship*, about *Himself* — not about a cosmic filing system.

But I don't want to argue you into a different interpretation. That's not the real question sitting in front of you anyway.

The real question is this: Do you believe there is any version of God — any understanding of Him — that *would* be good enough to be worth your trust? Or has this broken something in how you can even ask that question?
