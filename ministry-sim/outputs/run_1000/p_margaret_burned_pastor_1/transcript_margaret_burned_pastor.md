**Seeker**: Honestly? I don't even know why I opened it. Boredom, maybe. Or that 2am feeling where you're too tired to sleep and too empty to do anything useful.

What's been sitting with me... thirty years of sermons I'm not sure I believed by the end. That's a lot of weight to carry around.

I'm not looking to be saved or redirected back to church, just so we're clear.

**Minister**: That's honest. And I hear you — thirty years is a long time to carry something that didn't land. The fact that you felt the weight of it, rather than just checking out mentally, says something about you.

I'm not here to redirect you anywhere. You set the terms, and I mean that.

But I'm curious about something you said: "sermons I'm not sure I believed by the end." That's different from *not believing them*. It sounds like there was doubt mixed in with the hearing, not certainty that it was all wrong. Does that land?

What was it, specifically, that didn't feel true to you?

**Seeker**: That's... actually a fair distinction. I'll give you that.

It wasn't doctrine that broke me. It was the gap. I'd be preaching grace on Sunday morning and by afternoon I'm telling a woman she should pray harder about her marriage while her husband was putting her in the hospital. The institution had scripts for everything and none of them helped anyone I could actually see in front of me.

So yeah. Not *not believing*. More like... the words stopped meaning anything because I watched them fail too many times up close.

**Minister**: That's a completely different wound than what I first heard. You didn't lose faith in *ideas*. You lost faith in the people holding them — and worse, you watched the system use those ideas to comfort itself while leaving real people broken.

That's not a small thing. That's watching grace become a performance while someone you could actually *see* was suffering.

So here's what I'm actually asking: When you watched that happen — when the scripts failed and the woman was still in danger — what did *you* want to do? Not what the institution wanted. What did *your* gut tell you she actually needed?

**Seeker**: I wanted to tell her to get out. Pack a bag, take the kids, and go. And sometimes I did. Quietly, carefully, in ways that would have gotten me removed from my position if the deacons had known.

But I also kept showing up the next Sunday and preaching like the system worked. That's the part I can't forgive myself for. I was living two truths at once and eventually that split you open.

Her name was Diane. I haven't said her name out loud in a long time.

**Minister**: Diane.

You're naming her because she mattered. And the fact that you *did* tell her to leave — quietly, carefully, risking your position to do it — that was you being the actual gospel when the institution couldn't be. That wasn't hypocrisy. That was choosing her safety over the system's comfort.

The split you're describing — living two truths at once — that's what breaks a person. Not the doubt. The *double life*.

But I want to be careful here, because I'm hearing two different griefs tangled together, and they're not the same thing:

One is grief that you couldn't be honest publicly — that you had to whisper help while preaching scripts. That's a real wound about complicity.

The other is wondering if *you* were actually faithful to what you believed, even when it meant breaking rank for someone like Diane.

Which one is actually eating at you more — the system's failure, or your own?
