Specifically, whether a person can hold to anything less than full Calvinist double predestination and still have a logically coherent doctrine of grace. Because every alternative I've encountered either makes man the decisive factor in his own salvation — which is just works righteousness with a different label — or it collapses into some kind of divine wishful thinking where God *wants* to save people He somehow can't.

Romans 9 is right there. "Jacob I loved, Esau I hated, before they had done anything good or bad." That's not a description of God responding to foreseen faith. That's unconditional election or it's nothing.

But let me ask you something sharper than picking a side:

**When you read Romans 9 itself — not as a proof text for a system, but as Paul actually speaking — what is he actually trying to *accomplish* in his reader?** What's the thing he's fighting against? Because Paul's argument in 9-11 has a shape, and I'm not sure either pure framework perfectly catches what he's doing.

He's not defending *how* election works in the abstract. He's fighting Jewish exclusivism — the assumption that God's promises are exhausted in ethnic Israel. He's trying to *free* the Romans to see that God's mercy reaches further than they thought. And by 11, he's actively grieving over Israel and praying for their salvation.

That's a strange move for a determinist. That's a very strange move.

So: **what do you make of Paul's actual emotional posture in the chapter?** Does your framework have room for that, or does it have to explain it away?
