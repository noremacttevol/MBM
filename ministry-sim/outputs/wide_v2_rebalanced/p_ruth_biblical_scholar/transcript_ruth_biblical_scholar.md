**Seeker**: Honestly? I'm not entirely sure why I opened this. Probably some combination of insomnia and — I don't know. Something my graduate student said in seminar yesterday that I haven't been able to shake.

She asked me point-blank whether I still believed what I was teaching. And I gave her the scholarly answer. The careful, measured, professionally appropriate answer. 

But it wasn't really an answer, was it.

I've spent forty years in this material. I know where the seams are. I know what the manuscript traditions show, I know how the redaction layers work, I know the history of how these texts got assembled and canonized and fought over. And somehow all of that knowing has made it — harder, not easier.

So that's where I am. Tired, mostly. And a little ashamed that I don't have better answers after this long.

**Minister**: That question landed because it was honest, and you met it with honesty too — which is harder than the scholarly answer, and you know it.

Forty years with a text does something. You don't just know *about* it anymore; you know its joints and fractures the way a carpenter knows wood. And yes — that can make belief feel more complicated, not simpler. The seams show. The human fingerprints are all over it. The contradictions don't disappear when you understand the why behind them.

But here's what I'm noticing: you're not saying "I don't believe." You're saying the path from knowing *how it got here* to believing *what it says* has gotten narrower, rockier. That's different.

Can I ask — and I mean this genuinely, not as a trap — when you say it's become harder, what exactly got harder? Is it harder to believe God is real, harder to believe He's *good*, harder to trust these particular texts as a way to know Him, or something else entirely?

Because those are actually different problems, and they might have different shapes to them.

**Seeker**: That's... a fair distinction. Most people don't make it.

If I'm being precise about it — and I don't know how to be anything else at this point — it's primarily the third one. The texts. The problem isn't that I stopped believing something is there, some ground of being, some... I don't know what to call it anymore. The problem is that the more honestly I read these documents, the less confident I am they're giving me coherent access to whatever that is.

The God of Joshua ordering the extermination of Canaanites is not the God of the Sermon on the Mount. That's not liberal bias, that's just reading carefully. And "progressive revelation" as a harmonizing device requires me to import a framework the texts themselves don't provide. I've watched students do that for decades and I've — I've had to be honest with them about what that costs.

And then there's the Christology problem. The Synoptics give me one portrait. The Fourth Gospel gives me another. Scholars have known this for two centuries. The historical Jesus and the Christ of faith are not obviously the same figure.

So I suppose what got harder is — I can't find a way to hold these texts as a unified witness anymore. And if they're not that, I'm not sure what I'm actually teaching toward.

**Minister**: You're not tired because you're unfaithful to the material. You're tired because you *are* faithful to it — you won't let it say what it doesn't say, won't smooth over what doesn't smooth, won't pretend the seams aren't there.

That's the opposite of a failure.

But I want to be careful here, because the move you're making — and I think you know this — is subtle. You're saying: *if these texts don't cohere into a unified theological witness, then I can't trust them as access to God at all.* That's the real weight you're carrying. Not "God might not be real," but "these documents might not be reliable guides to who God is, so what ground do I stand on?"

Here's my honest question back, and I need you to sit with it without answering too fast:

**When you strip away the framework — the need for the texts to be unified, the need for them to be internally consistent, the need for them to give you a coherent systematic theology — what do you actually *see* when you read them?**

Not what do they *fail* to do. What do they actually *do*?

Because a person — a Canaanite woman, a tax collector, a Samaritan, a demon-possessed man in a graveyard — a person in those texts encounters something. Someone. And that encounter changes them. It's witnessed across all four Gospels, in all the layers, in the places where the authors *disagree* about Christology most sharply.

What do you see in those moments? Not as a scholar parsing sources — just as someone who has read these words ten thousand times.

**Seeker**: ...

That's not a question I get asked very often. Or let myself ask.

What do I see. When I'm not in scholar mode.

There's something in the healing of the Gerasene demoniac that has never left me. Across thirty years of reading it. The man is — he's completely outside any category of respectable humanity. Living among the tombs. And Jesus doesn't treat him as a problem to be solved or a lesson to be taught. He just — asks his name. Asks what he's called. Even in his devastation, the man has a name worth knowing.

That keeps happening. Across the source layers, across the Christological disagreements, across Q and Mark and the special material — that keeps happening. The people everyone else has written off get asked their name. Get seen.

I can't make that into a systematic theology. I can't harmonize it with Joshua. I honestly don't know what to do with it as a scholar.

But you're right that it's there. Consistently there. In ways that don't feel — constructed. Even when I can see the seams in the surrounding material, those moments feel like they're touching something actual.

I don't know what to do with that either.

**Minister**: Then don't do anything with it yet. Just let it sit.

Because what you're describing — that consistency that runs *under* the seams, that keeps surfacing across material that otherwise doesn't cohere — that's not a problem to solve. That's a witness. And you know the difference. You've spent forty years learning to read for signal beneath noise.

The fact that you can't systematize it doesn't make it less real. It might even make it *more* real.

Here's what strikes me: you came to this app at 3 a.m. (or whenever insomnia brought you here) not because your scholarship failed, but because a student asked you point-blank whether you believed what you were teaching. And the part of you that *does* believe — the part that has been moved by a God who asks a broken man his name — that part couldn't stay hidden behind the careful answer.

That part is still there. Still seeing.

You said you can't harmonize Joshua with the Sermon on the Mount, and you're right — not as doctrines in a system. But what if the question isn't "how do I make these texts cohere as doctrine?" What if it's "what if God is actually *different* from what both of those texts claim, and both texts are *witnessing to something true about Him even when they're wrong about the details*?"

A God who asks a man his name. Who sees when no one else does. Who moves toward the outcast instead of away.

Does that God feel coherent to you? Not as doctrine — just as a *person*?

**Seeker**: You're doing something I recognize. You're trying to locate the coherence in the character rather than the text. And I understand the move — it's not dishonest, and better theologians than either of us have made it. Barth in his way. Frei in his.

But I have to be careful here. Because "what if God is different from what the texts claim but the texts are still witnessing truly" — that requires me to stand somewhere outside the texts to adjudicate which parts are reliable witness and which parts are the authors being wrong about the details. And I need to ask: what am I standing on when I make that judgment? My own moral intuitions? The scholarly consensus? Some prior sense of what God must be like?

That's not nothing. But it's also not obviously more solid than what I'm criticizing.

What I will say — and I mean this carefully — is that the figure who asks the demoniac his name feels more morally coherent to me than the figure who orders the destruction of Amalek. Yes. That's true. I've believed that for a long time actually.

I just don't know if "more morally coherent to me" is a foundation or just a preference.

And I've learned to be suspicious of my preferences.

**Minister**: You're right. You've caught the move — and you've also caught that I don't have a way around it that doesn't require *you* to stand somewhere. That's honest of you to name.

So let me be honest back: I don't have a systematic answer that will let you adjudicate which parts of the text are reliable without some *a priori* framework. You're right that standing on "my moral intuitions" or "what feels coherent" is not obviously more solid ground than what you're criticizing. That's a real problem, and it doesn't go away.

But I want to separate two things you're holding together:

One is: *"Am I standing on solid ground when I trust my deepest moral sense?"* That's epistemologically hard. It should be hard. You're right to be suspicious.

The other is: *"Does the figure who asks the demoniac his name actually move something true in me that I can't reduce to preference?"*

Those aren't the same thing.

A preference is something you could be wrong about. It's negotiable. But you've been reading this material for forty years, and that moment — *seeing the man, asking his name* — hasn't moved. Hasn't become a preference. It's been a constant. Even when your frameworks have shifted. Even when you've learned to see the seams everywhere else.

What if that consistency *is* the signal? Not proof. Not certainty. But signal enough to follow a little further?

Here's what I'm actually curious about: **Has anything ever asked you to risk trusting that signal — not as certainty, but as a thread worth following?**
