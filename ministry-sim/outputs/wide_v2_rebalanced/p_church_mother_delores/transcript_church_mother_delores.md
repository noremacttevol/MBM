**Minister**: That's not just being human — that's being a grandparent who loves deeply. It means you're human *and* you love.

The fact that you can still say "I know He hasn't forgotten that boy" — after fifteen years — tells me something about who God is to you. You've learned Him through waiting.

Is it doubt about God's goodness, or is it more the ache of wanting to see your grandchildren safe and not being able to make it happen?

**Seeker**: That's a good question. And a fair one.

It's not doubt about God. No — I settled that a long time ago. The Lord has never left me, not through burying a husband, not through my own health troubles, not through things I don't talk about in public. He has always been faithful. That's not something I'm still working out.

What it is... is the ache. You put it right. It's watching Marcus make choices that are going to cost him, and not being able to stop it. It's sitting in church on Sunday and looking at that empty space where he used to sit when he was twelve years old and would sing with everything he had in him. That child had the Holy Ghost in him, I saw it. And now he thinks the church is irrelevant and God is something old people made up to cope.

That's not doubt. That's grief. There's a difference.

**Minister**: There is a difference. And you're naming it true.

That empty seat — that's not abstract theology. That's a real boy you watched sing with the Spirit alive in him, and now he's somewhere you can't reach him. That's something to grieve. The fact that you can still see *him* in that memory, still hold what was real about him then, doesn't make the loss smaller now. It makes it deeper.

And you're watching him walk toward pain he doesn't see coming yet. That's a particular kind of helplessness, isn't it? Not because God isn't faithful — you know He is — but because *you* can't make the boy see what you see. You can't force sight.

Fifteen years of prayer is not wasted. But I also hear that some days the weariness of *waiting* — of loving someone who's choosing away from you — catches up, and that's allowed to be hard.

Here's what I'm curious about, if you want to sit with it: In those moments when the ache is heaviest — when you're looking at that empty seat or thinking about the choices he's making — what do you actually *need* in that moment? Not what you pray *for* (which is his return). But what does your own heart need to hold onto, to keep going?

**Seeker**: *quiet for a moment*

You know, nobody asks me that. People ask me to pray for them. They come to me when they're hurting. That's my place and I don't resent it, it's a privilege. But nobody really asks what I need.

What I need... I think I need to know that God sees him the way I see him. Not what Marcus is right now, but who he is underneath all that hardness he's put on. I need to know the Lord looks at that boy and still sees the child who sang. That He hasn't written him off just because Marcus has written Him off.

And I think I do know that. I know the parable of the prodigal son. I've preached on it, I've taught on it. The father saw the boy coming from a long way off — that means he was looking. He was watching the road.

But knowing something in your head and needing to feel it in your chest when it's three in the morning — that's two different things.

**Minister**: Yes. That's exactly it.

And I want to tell you something: the fact that you *know* that parable in your head, and you still *need* it in your chest at three in the morning — that's not a failure of faith. That's the nature of love. You can know the Father is watching the road and still ache for the boy to come home. Both are true. Jesus wept at the tomb of Lazarus *knowing* He was about to raise him. He knew the ending. He still wept.

So at three in the morning, when the ache is sharpest and your head knowledge feels thin — you're allowed to need the comfort of it again. To need to *feel* that the Father hasn't stopped watching. To need to remember that Marcus, underneath all that hardness, is still the boy who sang.

And here's what strikes me: You already *know* what the Father sees. You see it too. You're the one still looking. You're the one who remembers. You're the one carrying the memory of who he was and trusting who he could be again. That's not nothing. In a real way, your faithful watching — your refusal to write him off — that's a reflection of how the Father watches.

Marcus doesn't see that yet. But the Lord does.

When you sit with that at three in the morning — not as theology, but as comfort — what happens in your chest?
