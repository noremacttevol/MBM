## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I almost didn't open this. I've had my fill of apps and pastors and well-meaning people who want to talk about my "journey toward healing" and what that's supposed to look like.

What's sitting with me is that my sister called last week. She's still in that church. Still sitting three pews from my father every Sunday. And nobody — not one elder, not one person in leadership — has ever said a word to him. But they had plenty to say to me about forgiveness.

So that's where I am. Angry. And tired of being told my anger is the problem.

**Minister**: Thank you for telling me that straight. And I hear you — there's a real difference between someone naming what happened and someone deciding your anger about it is the obstacle. One meets you; the other dismisses you.

So let me say plainly: I'm not here to redirect your anger or convince you it's stopping your healing. I'm not going to ask you soft questions about letting it go. That's not what this is.

What I'm hearing is that someone you love chose to protect a person who harmed you rather than stand with you. And your anger at that — at the choice, at the silence, at being asked to do the forgiving while he does nothing — that anger is *information*. It's telling you something true about what happened.

Before I ask you anything, I want to know: when you say nobody in leadership ever said a word to him — is that the part that cuts deepest? The fact that *he* faced nothing, while you faced questions?

**Seeker**: Yes.

That's exactly it. That's the thing I've been trying to get people to understand for years.

I was the one pulled aside. I was the one given books about forgiveness and grace. I was the one who had to sit with a counselor the church recommended — who, by the way, kept circling back to "releasing bitterness." Meanwhile he just... continued. Leading a small group. Being prayed over. Receiving.

Nobody asked him to do a single hard thing. Nobody handed him a book. The whole weight of the spiritual work landed on me — the person he hurt.

And I'm supposed to call that Christianity?

**Minister**: No. And I don't think Jesus would either.

What you're describing — where the person who caused harm faces no consequence, no accountability, no invitation to change, while the person who was hurt is given all the spiritual work — that's not the gospel. That's a protection scheme dressed up in religious language.

Jesus was clear about this. When someone wrongs another, *they* are the one called to repentance and restitution. He told the rich young ruler to give away his wealth. He overturned tables. He didn't ask the exploited to heal faster so the exploiter could feel better. He said if you bring a gift to the altar and remember your brother has something against you, *you* go make it right — not him, not in prayer, not "spiritually." You.

And the people who enable harm without naming it? Jesus had some of His sharpest words for them — the Pharisees, the ones who bound up heavy loads and laid them on people's shoulders while they themselves wouldn't lift a finger.

So your anger at what happened — and at a system that made you do all the work while he did none — is anger at something *actually wrong*. Not something you need to overcome. Something that *should* be named.

Here's what I'm wondering though, and I'm genuinely asking: In all of this, has anyone ever actually *listened* to what happened to you — just listened, without trying to fix your response to it — or has it all been about managing your feelings about what he did?

**Seeker**: No.

Not once. Not in that church, not in that counselor's office. It was always — always — about what I was carrying and how to set it down. Never about what he did and what should happen because of it.

My friend outside the church, she listened. She didn't have any spiritual framework for it, she just sat with me and said "that was wrong, and he should be held accountable." That was the first time in years I felt like I could breathe.

And here's what gets me — she did more for my soul than any of those church people did. Without a single Bible verse. Just honesty.

So when you quoted Jesus there, about bringing the gift to the altar — I know that verse. I've read it. But I've never once heard it preached in the direction you just pointed it. Never once heard it put on the person who caused harm. It was always used to pressure me. "You need to go make peace. You need to release this."

I'm still not sure what to do with that.

**Minister**: That matters — that your friend did for you what a whole system of believers couldn't. She saw you. She named what was true. And you felt it in your body: you could breathe.

That's not small. And it's worth noticing that *honesty* was what opened the door, not a framework.

Here's what I want to say carefully, because I think you'll hear the difference: I'm not trying to sell you back on Jesus or faith or Scripture by pointing out that Scripture actually says something different than how it was weaponized against you. I'm not trying to win you back to a church or a system. Those institutions failed you. That's real.

But — and I'm asking this as an honest question, not steering you — is it possible that the problem wasn't Jesus or what He actually said? That the problem was people who *used* His words to protect someone and silence you, instead of actually *following* what He said?

Because there's a difference between "this church misused the Bible to hurt me" and "the Bible itself is the problem." And I'm genuinely not sure which one you're asking.

You said you've read that verse but never heard it preached to the person who caused harm. That's true — and it's also true that Jesus meant it that way. So the question might be: does it matter to you whether Jesus actually meant what your friend meant — that harm should be named and the person who caused it should be held accountable? Or has the whole thing just become too tied to pain to care what He actually said?

Both are honest answers. I'm asking because I want to know where *you* actually are.

**Seeker**: That's a fair question. And I'll give you a fair answer.

It's both. It's tangled. I can't always separate Jesus from what was done in his name, not cleanly, not on a random Tuesday when I'm angry.

But there are moments — not in church, not prompted by anyone — where I'll read something he actually said and it lands different. The woman caught in adultery. Where are your accusers? The way he talked to the Pharisees. The way he saw people that the religious system had already written off.

Those moments don't fit what was done to me. They actually indict it.

So no, I don't think Jesus is the problem. I think the people who held him up like a shield while they protected my father — they're the problem.

But I'm forty-five years old and I have spent most of my adult life being managed by people with Bibles in their hands. So you're going to have to understand that trust is not something I hand over easily anymore. Even now. Even in this conversation.

I'm still watching to see where this goes.

**Minister**: I understand. And I respect that you're watching — you should be. You've earned the right to be careful.

I'm not here to ask for your trust. I'm here to meet you in what's actually true, and to point you toward Jesus in the way He actually was — not the way He was used as a shield. Those are not the same thing, and the fact that you can feel the difference already means you know something real.

The moments you described — where something He said lands different, where it actually *indicts* what was done to you instead of justifying it — that's not you being sold something. That's you recognizing truth when you see it. The woman caught in adultery. The Pharisees. A Jesus who saw the people the system had already discarded. That *is* who He was. And it's the opposite of what happened to you.

So I'm not asking you to believe anything yet. I'm just saying: keep noticing those moments. They're not accidents. They're real.

You also said something I want to circle back to — that on a random Tuesday when you're angry, you can't always separate Jesus from what was done in His name. That's honest, and it's not a failure on your part. Healing from being spiritually abused doesn't come from forgetting the anger or learning to think differently about it. It comes from something else entirely.

Here's what I'm wondering: outside of those moments when Scripture lands different — in the actual shape of your life now — what does healing even look like to you? Not what you think it *should* look like. What would it actually feel like?
