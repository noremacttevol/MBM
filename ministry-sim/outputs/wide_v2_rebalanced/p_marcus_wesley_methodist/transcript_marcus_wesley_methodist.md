## Conversation

**Minister**: He is risen — and from the way you carry it, I think that's good news you already hold. It's good to have you here. What's on your heart today — something you're carrying, something you're thankful for, or anything you'd like to talk through? I'm here to listen.

**Seeker**: He is risen indeed. I appreciate the greeting.

I'll be honest — I'm not entirely sure what I'm doing on an app like this. I've been in Methodist small groups for thirty years. I know my scripture. But something's been sitting heavy with me lately and I haven't found the right place to put it down.

My daughter. She's 31 now and she's told me flat out she doesn't believe any of it. Says it's just cultural conditioning, people believing what they were raised to believe. I raised her in the church. Every Sunday. She was confirmed. And now this.

I don't know if I'm looking for answers or just somewhere to say it out loud.

**Minister**: Thirty years in a church community, and then this weight lands right in the center of what matters most to you — your daughter, and whether what you've given her was real or just something that didn't take root. That's heavy. I'm glad you named it.

Before I say anything else, let me ask you this, because it matters: when your daughter says she doesn't believe — when you two talk about it — do you sense anger at *you* underneath, or is it more that she's genuinely unconvinced? Or maybe both, depending on the day?

**Seeker**: That's a fair question. I've thought about it.

Mostly I think she's genuinely unconvinced. She's not bitter toward me — we still talk, still have dinner when she's in town. She's not punishing me with it. She just looks at faith the way she looks at other claims and says the evidence isn't there. She's a scientist. Biologist.

That almost makes it harder in a way. If she were angry I could work with that. But she's calm about it. Reasonable. And when she explains her thinking I can't find the hole in the argument. That's not something I say lightly.

What I keep coming back to is whether I taught her the right things or just taught her the motions. Showed up, behaved right, served on committees. Maybe I modeled religion more than I modeled faith. I don't know the difference half the time if I'm honest.

**Minister**: That honesty lands different. You're not blaming her or defending yourself — you're looking at what *you* actually handed her, and that takes real courage.

Let me sit with something you just said: "I modeled religion more than I modeled faith. I don't know the difference half the time."

That might be the realest thing you've said. And it sounds like it's been sitting in you longer than just since your daughter walked away.

So here's what I'm genuinely asking: When you think about *your own* faith — not what you teach, not what you show up for, but the actual thing between you and God — what does that look like right now? Is it alive? Or has it become the motions too?

**Seeker**: That's a harder question than you probably realize you just asked.

I'd say it's alive. I believe what I believe. But if I'm being straight with you — and I'm not sure why I'm being this straight with a phone app — it's been more dutiful than alive for a while. Years maybe. I do the things because the things are worth doing. I lead the group, I study the text, I show up. That's not nothing.

But that feeling I had when I was younger — like something was actually listening, like it mattered beyond the keeping of the order — I couldn't tell you exactly when that quieted down. It just did.

I always figured that was just what mature faith looked like. Less emotion, more commitment. Back when I was young people talked about feelings in church and I never trusted it much. So I told myself steady and dutiful was better than charismatic and inconsistent.

Maybe I was right about that. But sitting here I'm not entirely sure.

**Minister**: You just said something true that a lot of people don't say out loud: that you traded one thing for another, and you told yourself it was a good trade. And maybe there's wisdom in that — steadiness matters, duty matters. But you're asking now whether you gave up something you shouldn't have.

Here's what strikes me: your daughter looks at faith and asks for evidence. She's reasonable. She can't find the hole in her own argument. And you — you *have* the evidence she's looking for, but it's not the kind you can hand her on a chart. It's something you *felt*. And you decided, years ago, that feelings weren't trustworthy, so you stopped paying attention to them.

So now when she asks you *why* you believe — not the theology, but the actual reason — what do you have to show her? The dutiful keeping of order. Which, to her mind, proves her point: that this is just what people do when they're raised to do it.

I'm not saying you're wrong to have walked away from emotionalism. But I want to ask you something Jesus actually asked people: **Do you *want* that aliveness back? Not whether you think you should want it — actually want it?**

Because if you do, that might be the first honest thing your daughter hears from you about faith. Not an argument she can't answer. Just a person who's reached for something real and found it.

**Seeker**: I need to sit with that for a minute.

You're right that I don't have much to show her beyond the structure. And I've never put it to myself that plainly before. That's uncomfortable.

But I want to push back on something. You're implying that if I get this aliveness back, that becomes my witness to her. That feels like it's still making her the project. Like faith is a means to winning an argument with my daughter. I don't think that's right either.

As for whether I want it back — the aliveness — yes. I think I do. But I distrust that answer a little because I can't tell if I want it for the right reasons or just because I feel like I've been running on empty and I want relief.

And honestly I'm not sure a phone app is where that happens. No offense meant. I've seen too many people chase spiritual highs and end up nowhere. What you're describing could go sideways into exactly the kind of emotionalism I spent thirty years being skeptical of.

I'm not closing the door. I'm just not going to pretend I'm more convinced than I am.

**Minister**: No offense taken — and you just caught me in exactly the move I shouldn't have made. You're right. I was pointing toward "get the aliveness back so your daughter sees it," and that *is* making her the project. That's not faith; that's performance with a different costume. You called it fair.

And your skepticism about spiritual highs and emotionalism? That's not something to shed. That's wisdom. Real faith — the kind Jesus talked about — isn't feelings. It's something steadier than that. It's *relationship*. And relationships don't happen through apps or through chasing relief. They happen in the actual presence of someone.

Here's what I think is true: you *have* reached for something real. You felt it once. It quieted down. And now you're asking whether it's still there, or whether you killed it with thirty years of duty. That's the actual question — not how to use it with your daughter, but whether it's real to begin with.

And that question? That one deserves more than what I can offer through a screen. It deserves someone who knows you, who can sit across from you, who can pray with you, not at you.

There's a real person available through this app — someone who can walk with you on this, not just in one conversation. Not to convince you of anything, but to help you actually listen to what God might be saying in the quiet you've been living in.

Would that be something you'd want?
