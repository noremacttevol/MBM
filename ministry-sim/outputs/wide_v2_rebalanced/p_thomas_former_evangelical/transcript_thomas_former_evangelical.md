## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I'm not sure why I opened this. Probably boredom mixed with some kind of morbid curiosity — like poking at a scar to see if it still hurts.

What's been sitting with me is this low-grade thing I can't quite shake. I spent most of my twenties completely certain about something that I now think was... historically constructed. And I don't regret leaving, intellectually I know I made the right call. But there's this weird grief that nobody really talks about — like, the community, the sense of meaning, even the rituals. I miss the *feeling* without believing any of it anymore.

Which is probably just neurology. The brain likes patterns and belonging. That's all it was.

**Minister**: That's honest, and I hear the weight in it — that specific kind of loneliness where you *know* you made the right call intellectually, and the grief still shows up anyway. Those two things can absolutely live in the same person at the same time.

And yeah, neurology is real. The brain does crave pattern and belonging. But I'd gently push back on the "that's all it was" — because missing meaning and community isn't just a neurological glitch you outgrow. Missing those things *means* something. It means you're someone who hungers for real connection, for something that matters, for a reason to gather with others who see the world the way you do. That hunger is actually pointing to something true about you, not something broken.

The question isn't whether the feelings are neurological (they are) — it's whether they're *only* that, or whether they're also saying something real about what you actually need.

Can I ask — when you say you left something you were certain about, are you completely certain *now* that nothing of it was true? Or is it more that you saw the construction and couldn't unsee it, so certain doesn't feel possible anymore in either direction?

(And no pressure to answer that. Just wondering where you actually land.)

**Seeker**: That's... actually a fair distinction. I'll give you that.

And no, I'm not certain in the other direction. That would be the same mistake in reverse. What I have is — the historical record is what it is. The Council of Nicaea happened. The canon was contested and politically shaped. Constantine had very specific reasons for wanting a unified church. None of that is fringe scholarship, it's just what happened, and most people in the pews have no idea.

So it's less "I know there's no God" and more "the specific vehicle I was handed for getting to God was demonstrably human-made, top to bottom." And once you see that, you can't really unsee it. The whole edifice was built on this assumption that the Bible just *appeared*, that the early church was this pure untouched thing. It wasn't.

The grief isn't about God exactly. It's more about — I genuinely don't know what to do with the Jesus stuff. The historical Jesus question is actually interesting to me intellectually. But I can't go back to the version I had. That guy was invented by committees.

**Minister**: You've done real work here — the kind that takes intellectual honesty most people avoid their whole lives. And you're right: those things *did* happen. Councils shaped doctrine. Politics shaped canon. That's history, not heresy to name it.

But I want to set something beside that, because I think you're carrying two separate questions that feel like one.

**The first:** Did humans shape the Bible and early doctrine? Yes. Absolutely yes. Councils met, decisions were made, politics mattered. That's all true.

**The second:** Does that mean the Jesus in those pages is therefore invented by committees?

Here's where I'd push gently: you're assuming those two things are the same question, and I'm not sure they are.

Think about it this way — yes, the canon was contested and shaped by human hands and human agendas. But the *earliest* Jesus material — the letters of Paul, written in the 50s, maybe 20 years after the crucifixion — those were written *before* any council, before any political maneuvering around orthodoxy. Paul wasn't trying to invent Jesus; he was trying to explain someone who had already turned his life upside down and gotten him killed.

Does the *fact* that humans shaped the Bible mean everything in it is therefore invented? Or could it mean humans carried something real through their very human, very political hands?

What would it mean if the Jesus who shows up in those earliest sources — the one who loved the wrong people, who claimed authority only God could claim, who got executed for it — what if *that* guy is actually harder to explain as committee-invention than as something that actually happened?

Does that question even interest you, or does the historical mess make it feel too contaminated to touch?

**Seeker**: Okay, the Paul thing is legitimate. I've read Ehrman on this, and even he doesn't dispute that Paul's letters are early and that Paul genuinely believed something happened to him. That's not the issue.

The issue is the gap between "Paul had a transformative experience" and "therefore the resurrection accounts in the gospels are reliable history." Those gospels were written decades later, in different communities, with demonstrably different theological agendas. Mark's Jesus is not John's Jesus. That's not me being cynical, that's just textual criticism.

And yes, the historical Jesus question interests me. That's the part I can't cleanly dismiss. There's clearly *someone* at the center of this — a first-century Jewish apocalypticist who said genuinely strange and interesting things. Some of the sayings have that quality of... I don't know, they don't read like committee work. The stuff about loving enemies, the kingdom belonging to the poor. That material has a texture to it.

But "interesting historical figure with radical ethics" is a very long way from "second person of the Trinity." That's where I lose the thread completely. That leap feels like exactly the kind of thing a politically motivated council would construct.

**Minister**: Now you're asking the real question — and it's a good one, because you're not dodging the mess; you're actually sitting in it.

You're right that Mark's Jesus is not John's Jesus on the surface. But here's what interests me about your last point: you said some of the sayings have a texture that doesn't read like committee work. That *texture* — that's your intuition picking up on something. So let me ask this carefully:

**What if the different theological agendas in those gospels aren't evidence that someone invented Jesus, but evidence that several different people encountered the same person and were trying to *explain* him from their own angle?**

Think about witnesses to an accident. They all saw the same event. Their accounts differ — one focuses on what he said, one on what he did, one on the emotional impact, one on the timeline. Does the difference mean the accident didn't happen? Or does it mean real people, with real perspectives, were trying to capture something that was bigger than any single angle could contain?

Mark's Jesus and John's Jesus *do* look different. But they agree on something wild: this guy claimed to forgive sins (a power only God had), claimed authority over the Sabbath (same thing), claimed people's eternal fate depended on how they responded to *him* — not to the law, not to the temple, to *him*. 

That's not a disagreement between the gospels. That's a constant. And it's the part that would get you killed in first-century Judaism. So here's what I'm asking:

**If someone was going to invent a politically useful messiah in the first century, why invent one who makes these claims that are almost guaranteed to get him executed and make your new movement illegal?** 

Why not invent a figure who's more... useful? More domesticated?

What do you make of that?

**Seeker**: That's the argument C.S. Lewis was making, and I know it well. The "Lord, Liar, or Lunatic" framework. It's rhetorically elegant but it has problems — mainly that it assumes the words attributed to Jesus were actually said by Jesus, which is precisely what's in dispute. You can't use the gospel accounts to prove the gospel accounts.

And the "why invent someone inconvenient" argument — I mean, that's interesting but it cuts both ways. Movements regularly coalesce around inconvenient martyrs precisely *because* the martyrdom gives them power. The execution becomes the point. That's not evidence against invention, it might actually be evidence for a very sophisticated narrative construction.

But I'll be honest — the claims you're highlighting, the forgiveness of sins, the Sabbath authority — some of that does sit oddly with me. Because you're right that a first-century Jewish apocalypticist making those claims isn't operating within any normal category. Either he's deluded, or something stranger is happening.

I just... I can't get past the institutional Christianity that got built on top of whatever that original thing was. The Crusades. The Inquisition. The abuse cover-ups happening right now. If this is the fruit, I have serious questions about the tree.

And that's not deflection — that's the actual thing I carry. The historical Jesus might be genuinely interesting. The church that claimed him is genuinely monstrous in places.

**Minister**: You're doing something important here — you're separating two things that most people glue together, and refusing to let the answer to one question automatically answer the other.

That's not deflection. That's intellectual integrity.

So let me be direct: **you're right about the institutional church. Catastrophically, repeatedly, provably right.** The Crusades happened. The Inquisition happened. Abuse is happening now. Those aren't regrettable footnotes — they're part of the historical record of what people claiming to follow Jesus have done in his name. And someone with any moral sense *should* have serious questions about that.

But here's where I want to press you back, gently:

**Does the monstrousness of the institution prove anything about whether the original thing — the person at the center — was true or false?**

Because you're carrying two separate griefs. One is "I can't trust the vehicle that carried this to me." The other is "even if the person was real and radical, what got built in his name is monstrous, so what's the point?"

Those aren't the same question. And the second one — I think that one is actually about God's character, not about historical evidence.

Here's what I mean: If Jesus actually *was* who he claimed — if he actually did forgive sins and rise from the dead — then his monstrousness isn't proof that he wasn't. It's proof that humans are capable of betraying even the clearest, most direct moral teaching. Which... we already knew.

The question underneath might be: **If God is real and good, why does He let people claim His name and do monstrous things in it? And if He does, what does that say about Him?**

Is that closer to what's actually sitting with you?
