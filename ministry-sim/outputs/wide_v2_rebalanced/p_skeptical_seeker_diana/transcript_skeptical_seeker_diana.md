## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I'm not entirely sure why I opened this. 

My mother passed eight months ago. She was... she had this faith that I could never understand, and I spent most of my adult life thinking she was wrong about it. Now she's gone and I keep catching myself wondering if *she* knew something I don't. Which is probably just grief talking.

I'm not here looking to be converted. I want to be clear about that upfront. But I'm 54 years old and there's this persistent — I don't know — *wistfulness*, I suppose, about what it might feel like to believe something the way she did. She had a kind of peace I've never managed to find through reason alone.

So. That's the honest answer.

**Minister**: Thank you for telling me that. That's the kind of honesty that matters, and I hear it — all of it. The loss of her, the watching her have something you couldn't quite reach, and now the wondering without any pressure to decide what the wondering means yet.

Eight months is still so fresh. Grief doesn't follow a timeline.

I want to honor something you just said: you're right that some of what you're feeling is grief. But I'd gently push back on the idea that it's *only* grief talking. A person can miss their mother *and* genuinely sense that she understood something true about how to live. Those aren't opposites. Sometimes grief clarifies what we actually value in someone — and what we miss isn't just them, it's the way they moved through the world. The peace you watched her carry, even if you couldn't name where it came from, was real. That wistfulness might be grief, yes — but it might also be you, actually noticing something.

Here's what I won't do: I won't try to convince you into what she believed. That would be the opposite of respect for both of you.

But I will say this — if you want to understand what gave *her* that peace, that's a real question, and it's worth asking. Not to become her, but to understand her.

What was the faith she held? And when you watched her live it — when you saw that peace you're describing — what did it actually look like in her day to day?

**Seeker**: She was Catholic. Quietly so — she didn't wear it loudly, which I actually respected. She prayed every morning, lit candles, went to Mass. When things were hard she had this... steadiness. Not naivety. She knew life was difficult. She just seemed to have somewhere to put it.

What I couldn't reconcile was that she was also brilliant. She read widely, she was a scientist actually — a botanist — and she held both things without apparent contradiction. That always bothered me, if I'm honest. Not her faith, exactly, but that I could never figure out how she did it. How a rigorous mind could also just... accept things that can't be verified.

I asked her once. She said something like "Diana, the most important things can't be put under a microscope." 

I didn't have a response to that at the time. I still don't know if it's wise or if it's a dodge.

**Minister**: I think your mother gave you a truer answer than she may have known — and it's worth sitting with, not dismissing.

She wasn't saying reason doesn't matter. A botanist knows better than almost anyone that the world *demands* rigorous attention. She was saying something more precise: that the deepest questions — about meaning, about who to trust, about how to live when things break — those can't be resolved by the method alone. A microscope is brilliant for what it's made for. But it can't tell you whether life is worth living. It can't tell you whether you're loved. It can't tell you whether to forgive, or how to grieve your daughter well.

The real question isn't whether reason and faith contradict. It's whether a person can hold both — and your mother did, plainly. She didn't sacrifice her mind. She used it *and* she trusted something her mind alone couldn't prove.

That's not a dodge. That's actually how grown-up faith usually works — at least the kind that holds steady when things are genuinely hard.

Here's what I'm curious about, though — and tell me if this lands or misses: When you watched her have that steadiness, that somewhere to put it... do you think what you're actually missing is the answer she had? Or is it the *capacity* to trust something without needing to verify it first? Because those are different longings, and they matter.

Which one feels closer to true for you?

**Seeker**: That's... a sharper question than I expected.

I need to think about that honestly rather than just deflecting. 

I think it might be the capacity. Because I'm aware that even if someone gave me a logically airtight argument for God's existence tomorrow, I'm not sure that would translate into what she had. What she had wasn't a conclusion she'd reasoned her way to. It was something she *lived in*. And I don't know how you get there from where I am.

That's actually the part that feels most impossible to me. Not the intellectual questions, though I have plenty of those. It's more like — I can see the destination from here but I have no idea if there's a path that starts from my particular location. Fifty-four years of a certain way of being in the world. A mind trained toward skepticism. 

How do you cross that kind of distance? And I'm asking that as a genuine question, not a rhetorical one. I'm not sure it's crossable for someone like me.

**Minister**: You've just named the real thing, Diana. Not whether God exists — whether *you* could ever actually live differently than you do. Whether the distance is crossable from *here*. That's braver than most people get to.

And I want to be honest with you: I don't think the crossing happens the way you're imagining it. You're picturing a logical argument that then translates into living — a conclusion that becomes a capacity. But your mother didn't work that way, and I don't think that's how it works for anyone who actually changes.

It usually works backwards.

People don't reason their way into trust and then live it. They *live* into it — they take one small step that feels dangerous, they notice what happens, they take another. They pray without being sure anyone is listening. They forgive someone and find their own chest becomes lighter. They put down the need to control or verify everything, just once, and discover they don't break. Slowly, a new way of being emerges — not from solving the intellectual problem, but from actually *moving* differently.

Your mother didn't start with a proof. She started with something simpler: willingness. Maybe it was inherited, maybe it was grace, maybe both. But at some point she decided to trust something she couldn't verify, and *then* she lived from that. The capacity grew from the living, not before it.

So here's the real question underneath your question: If you're not waiting for certainty — if you're willing to admit certainty may never come the way you want it — what would it look like to take one small step *as if*? Not to believe fully yet. Just to... try moving like she moved, and see what you notice?

What would feel possible to try?

**Seeker**: I appreciate the honesty in that response. And I notice I'm not immediately dismissing it, which is something.

But I have to be careful here. "Act as if" has always struck me as a form of self-deception. If I don't believe something, pretending I do feels — intellectually dishonest. Like I'd be manufacturing a feeling rather than arriving at a truth.

Though I'll grant you this much: I do understand that some things are only knowable through participation. You can't understand grief without grieving. You can't understand parenthood without becoming a parent. Maybe there's an analogy there.

What I keep coming back to is — what exactly would I be stepping toward? My mother had a specific tradition, specific beliefs, a specific figure at the center of it. I can't just step toward "spirituality in general." That feels like vapor. She believed actual things. Concrete things.

So if I'm honest about what makes the distance feel uncrossable — it's not the practice. It might be the object. The thing itself that's being asked to be trusted. That's where I get stuck every time.

**Minister**: You've just cut to the actual bone, Diana. Not the method — the *object*. That matters, and I respect that you won't settle for vapor.

And you're right to be cautious about self-deception. That's not a flaw in you; that's integrity. So I won't ask you to act-as-if your way to belief. That's not what I meant anyway.

But I want to separate two things you're holding together:

One is: "I can't just manufacture feeling." That's true and non-negotiable — you can't fake your way into genuine trust.

The other is: "Some things are only knowable through participation." That's also true. And it doesn't require you to fake anything. It just means you can't *know* from the outside whether the object is trustworthy. You can only know by moving toward it and paying attention to what actually happens.

Your mother didn't start by feeling the peace. She started by praying. By going to Mass. By putting down the need to verify and *noticing what happened in that space*. The peace came after — or maybe it came *through* — the participation, not before.

So here's where I need to actually answer your real question: What is the object? What did your mother actually believe in, trust in?

Because you're right — you can't step toward vapor. But before we talk about whether you *could* step toward what she believed, I need to understand: Do you actually know what it was? Not the doctrine. The person. The presence. The reality she was turning toward each time she lit a candle.

What was at the center of it for her, as far as you could tell?
