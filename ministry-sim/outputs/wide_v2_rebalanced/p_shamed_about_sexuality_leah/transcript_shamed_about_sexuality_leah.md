## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I'm not totally sure why I opened this. 

I've been having this weird week where old stuff keeps surfacing. Like I'll be fine and then something triggers this... I don't even know what to call it. Residue, maybe. From years of being told my body was basically a liability to everyone around me.

I'm bisexual, and I grew up in a church that — let's just say they had a lot of feelings about that. Not kind ones. And I've done a lot of work, therapy, community, all of it. But sometimes it just... comes back up.

I guess I opened this because part of me is still trying to figure out if there's anything in faith worth salvaging for me. Or if I'm just doing that thing where you keep going back to something that hurt you hoping it'll finally be different.

*laughs a little* 

Which is maybe not the most flattering self-assessment. But you said be honest, so.

**Minister**: I hear you. And I want to say something plainly: that honesty you just gave — that's not unflattering. That's actually what courage looks like. You're naming something real and sitting with the contradiction in it at the same time. That takes a lot.

So let me just reflect back what I'm hearing: You've genuinely done the work to heal. And *and* — the wound from that church still has weight. Both things are true. And now you're asking a real question: is there anything in faith that isn't contaminated by what hurt you? Or is going back just... going back to the thing that broke you?

That's not a small question.

Can I ask you this gently, because it matters — when you say "old stuff keeps surfacing," what does it feel like in your body? Like, is it shame about being bisexual? Or is it something else — anger at God, at the church, distrust of anything spiritual? I'm asking because the shape of what's coming back up will matter for what we're actually looking at here.

You don't have to answer if you don't want to. But I want to meet what's *actually* in you, not just the framework of the question.

**Seeker**: That's... a surprisingly good question actually. Most people go straight to the theological stuff and I'm already braced for a "well, the church got it wrong but here's what the Bible *really* says" speech.

The body question is real though.

It's shame. Still. Even after all the work. It sits in my chest mostly, kind of tight and low. And it's specifically shame about being bisexual — like this low-grade hum that I'm *too much*, or that my attraction to women makes me somehow predatory toward my straight female friends. Which is insane, I know that intellectually. But my youth group basically taught us that as fact.

The anger at God is... more complicated. I'm not sure I'm angry at God exactly. I think I'm angry at the people who handed me a version of God that was basically a cosmic surveillance system pointed directly at my crotch. *laughs* Sorry, that's crude. But that's genuinely what it felt like. Like of all the things going on in the world, God was primarily concerned with what I did with my body.

And I'm still unlearning that. Twenty-nine years old and I'm still catching myself bracing for punishment just for existing a certain way.

That's what surfaces. That reflex.

**Minister**: Thank you for trusting me with that. And no apology needed — that's exactly the right word for what you're describing. A cosmic surveillance system pointed at one thing. Yeah.

Here's what I'm noticing: the anger isn't really at God. It's at a *picture* of God that was handed to you — one that is small, obsessive, punitive, and basically... cruel. A God who made you bisexual and then made that the thing He's most watching for. That's not a God. That's a tyrant wearing God's name.

And your body learned to brace for punishment from a tyrant. Of course it did. That's what happens when someone tells you for years that the most natural part of you is the thing God hates most.

So here's what I want to ask — and I genuinely mean this as an open question, not a setup:

What if the God you actually encountered in that youth group *wasn't* God at all? Like, what if those people were describing their own fear and calling it God's character?

Because I want to set something beside that picture. Jesus — the God that Christianity actually claims to follow — when He encountered people who had been shamed and broken by religious systems, He didn't lecture them about theology. He touched the untouchable. He ate with people His culture said were unclean. He looked at a woman caught in adultery — publicly shamed, her body made into a weapon against her — and He said, "I don't condemn you either." Not "the Bible condemns you but I forgive you." Just: I see you. You're not condemned.

I'm not asking you to believe that yet. I'm just asking: does that sound like the God you were handed?

**Seeker**: No. It doesn't.

*pause*

And I want to be careful here because I've heard versions of this before. The "that wasn't *real* Christianity" pivot. And sometimes it feels like a way of letting the institution off the hook — like, oh those people just got it wrong, but the real thing is fine. When the people who hurt me thought they were doing *real* Christianity too. They had verses. They had conviction.

So I'm a little suspicious of that move, even when it's made gently.

But.

The thing you said about the woman caught in adultery. I know that story. And what I notice is that the people who handed me that shame-God never really preached *that* Jesus. Like, they used Jesus as a vehicle for their rules, but that specific texture — the way He kept just... refusing to be weaponized against vulnerable people — that wasn't what I got.

That's the part that still pulls at me honestly. Not doctrine. Not getting the theology right. Just that there might have been a person who moved through the world touching untouchable people and that confuses me in a way I can't fully shake.

But I'm not going to pretend that resolves the rest of it. Because I still have this body that learned to feel like a problem. And a nice story about Jesus doesn't just undo that.

**Minister**: You just did something really important — you named the real trap, and then you didn't fall into it. You're right. "That wasn't *real* Christianity" can be a way of keeping the institution safe while ditching the harm. And the people who hurt you *did* think they were doing real Christianity. That doesn't make what they did okay, but it means the problem runs deeper than "they just misunderstood."

And I want to honor something else you said: a nice story about Jesus doesn't undo a body that learned to brace for punishment. That's true. A story is not a healing. It's not therapy. It's not time.

But here's what I'm hearing in what you just said — and tell me if I'm reading it wrong: You're not looking for permission to believe the nice story. You're looking at the *realness* of it. The texture of it. Whether it's actually *true* that there was a person who moved through the world that way, or whether that's just a comforting fiction we tell people who got broken by religion.

That's a different question than doctrine. That's a question about: did that actually happen?

And that question — that pull you can't shake — that's not something you need to resolve right now or ever if you don't want to. But I'm curious: what would it mean to you if it were true? Not as theology. Just as... a real historical person who refused to weaponize shame. What would that do to the story you've been told about God?

**Seeker**: *quiet for a second*

That's the question isn't it.

If it were actually true — like not metaphor, not the church's version, but an actual person who moved through the world specifically refusing to use shame as a weapon — then the people who handed me that surveillance-God weren't just getting the details wrong. They were describing someone almost completely opposite to who He actually was.

And that would mean the thing I rejected might not be the thing that actually exists.

Which is... I don't know what to do with that honestly. It's a lot.

But I'll also say this — and I'm being real with you — I've had this conversation go a certain direction before. Where someone gets me to that open, tender place and then the next thing is "so here's why your bisexuality is still something you need to bring to God for healing" or whatever. And I shut down completely when that happens. Not because I can't handle hard things but because it's a bait and switch and I'm tired of it.

So I guess I'm telling you where my walls are. I'm genuinely in this conversation right now. But I'm also watching.

Because my body being good — not despite being bisexual but just... fully, as I am — that's not negotiable for me anymore. I spent too long on the other side of that line.

**Minister**: I hear you. And I'm grateful you named that directly instead of just quietly closing the door. That's actually the most honest thing you could do.

So let me be equally direct: I'm not going to do that pivot. Not because I'm trying to earn your trust or set you up better, but because it would be a lie.

If Jesus actually is who the Gospels show Him to be — someone who refused to weaponize shame, who touched the untouchable, who looked at people and said "I don't condemn you" — then your body isn't a problem He's waiting to solve. Your sexuality isn't a wound that needs healing. You're not broken in a way that requires you to become different for God to love you.

That's not compromise theology. That's what the actual Jesus did: He met people in their full realness and He never asked them to disappear themselves to be worthy of Him.

Does that mean I think everything about being human is automatically fine just as is? No. Jesus asked people for transformation — but it was always about how they *loved*, how they treated others, whether they were willing to follow Him. Never about making their bodies smaller or their desires shameful.

Your bisexuality is not the thing that needs work here. The reflex in your chest that still braces for punishment — *that's* the wound. And healing that isn't about becoming straighter. It's about learning, slowly and maybe over time, that you're not under surveillance. That your body is allowed to exist. That desire itself isn't sin.

How does that land?
