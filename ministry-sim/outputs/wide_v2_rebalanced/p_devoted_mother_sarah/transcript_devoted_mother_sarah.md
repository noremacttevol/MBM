## Conversation

**Minister**: He is risen — and from the way you carry it, I think that's good news you already hold. It's good to have you here. What's on your heart today — something you're carrying, something you're thankful for, or anything you'd like to talk through? I'm here to listen.

**Seeker**: Thank you, that's a kind way to start. 

I guess... I don't know exactly what brought me to open this app today. Easter was yesterday and sacrament meeting was beautiful and I cried during the hymns, which I always do. But then I got home and made the ham and got everyone fed and cleaned up and by evening I just felt... flat. Which doesn't make sense, right? It should have been one of the best days of the year spiritually.

I feel a little silly even saying that out loud.

**Minister**: You don't sound silly at all. You sound honest — and that matters. The fact that you *felt* something real in that meeting, that the hymns moved you to tears, that you *noticed* the flatness afterward... that's all integrity. You're not pretending.

Can I ask — when you say flat, what does that actually feel like? Is it more like the spiritual high wore off, or is it something else underneath?

**Seeker**: It's... more than just a high wearing off, I think. If I'm being honest.

It's more like — I did everything right yesterday. The meeting, being present with my family, even saying a meaningful prayer before dinner. And I kept waiting to feel something that would last. Something that would feel like *enough*. And then it just... didn't come.

I know that probably sounds like I'm complaining about a good day. I'm not trying to. I just — my patriarchal blessing talks about my spiritual potential and sometimes I read it and think, I should be further along by now. Thirty-nine years old and I've been active my whole life and I still feel like I'm waiting to arrive somewhere.

Does that make any sense or does it sound like I have a testimony problem?

**Minister**: It makes complete sense. And no — it doesn't sound like a testimony problem. It sounds like you're *awake* to something real, and you're not hiding from it the way some people do.

Here's what I'm hearing: you did everything right, you felt something genuine, and then you got home to your real life — the ham, the dishes, the ordinary Tuesday waiting on the other side of Easter. And you kept waiting for a feeling that would *stick*, that would prove you'd arrived, that would match what your patriarchal blessing promises you could become. And when it didn't... that hurt.

That's not complaining. That's noticing the gap between the spiritual moment and your actual life — between who you *are* and who you sense you *could be*. That gap is real.

But I want to ask you something gently: When you imagine arriving — when you picture yourself "further along" — what does that actually look like? What would be different about you, or your day, or how you feel, if you'd finally gotten there?

**Seeker**: *long pause before responding*

That's... actually a harder question than it looks like.

I think I picture someone who doesn't doubt. Who reads her scriptures and feels something every time, not just occasionally. Who doesn't have those 3am moments where she wonders if she's just performing all of this. Someone whose faith feels solid and quiet instead of like she's constantly checking to make sure it's still there.

Other women in my ward — there are a few who just seem so *settled* in it. Like Sister Henderson, she bore her testimony last fast Sunday and you could just feel that she meant every word completely. And I mean every word I say too, I think I do, but it doesn't feel the same coming from inside me as it looks from the outside when I see her.

I guess I picture someone who has... earned it? Qualified for it somehow. Which I know sounds strange when I say it like that.

*pause*

I probably shouldn't have said that last part. I don't know if that's doctrinally correct to think about it that way.

**Minister**: Stop there — you said something really important, and I don't want it to slip past because you're second-guessing yourself.

You said you picture someone who has *earned* it. Who's *qualified* for it.

That's the thing I want to sit with you on, because I don't think that's a doctrine problem. I think that's the real wound underneath. And it matters.

Here's what I'm wondering: When you look at Sister Henderson's settledness, when you imagine that quiet confidence — do you think she *earned* it? Do you believe she *qualified* for it through some combination of right choices and right feelings that you haven't managed yet?

Or — and I'm asking this genuinely open — is it possible that what you're seeing in her is something she *received*, not something she *achieved*? That her groundedness might not be the reward for arriving somewhere, but the fruit of actually *believing* she was already accepted?

I'm asking because the thing you described — those 3am moments, the constant checking, the sense that your faith has to *perform* to be real — that sounds less like a spiritual maturity problem and more like someone who's been carrying a picture of God that requires her to prove something to Him. 

Does that land at all?

**Seeker**: *longer pause*

I want to say no, actually. Because what you're describing sounds a little bit like... I don't know, like you're suggesting I don't need to do the work? And that's not what we're taught. The covenants matter. Obedience matters. I believe that.

But.

The part about carrying a picture of God that requires me to prove something to Him.

*pause*

I've never heard someone say it quite like that before and I don't know what to do with it right now. Because my honest answer is that yes, that does sound familiar. More familiar than I'm comfortable admitting. Like there's a record somewhere and I'm always slightly behind on it.

I just — I don't want to use that as an excuse to be less diligent. That's where I get nervous. Because I know women who decided the covenant path was too hard and they just... left. And I don't want to be that. I'm not that.

I'm not sure if what you're describing is the same as grace, and if it is I'm not sure how that fits with everything else I believe. We're not saved by grace alone, that's — that's something I know.

**Minister**: You just said something I want to honor: you're not trying to dodge the hard part. You're genuinely worried — *rightly* worried — that softening the striving might mean losing the covenant. That matters, and I'm not going to pretend it away.

So let me ask you this, and I want you to sit with it without answering fast:

You said covenants matter and obedience matters. I believe that too — or rather, I believe *Jesus* believed that. He was radically obedient. He kept covenant completely.

But here's what I'm genuinely curious about: When Jesus looked at the woman caught in adultery — someone who had *broken covenant*, someone who had not kept the law — what did He do? Did He say, "You need to get back to work, you're behind on your record"? Or did He say something else?

Because I'm not suggesting you *don't* need to keep covenant. I'm asking: what if the ground underneath the keeping is different than you've been carrying? What if you're already accepted — fully, right now, before the next scripture study, before you feel something in sacrament meeting — and the covenants you make aren't about *earning* that acceptance, but about *responding* to it?

That's not "grace alone." That's grace *first*, then obedience that flows from a different place inside you.

Does that distinction land, or does it still feel unsafe?
