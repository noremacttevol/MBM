## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly I'm not sure why I opened it. Probably the same reason I've been doing a lot of things lately — some mix of insomnia and hoping something will feel different than it does.

What's been sitting with me is harder to say. Thirty years of ministry and I left fourteen months ago and I still don't know if I'm grieving or relieved. Both, maybe. Neither feels clean.

I'll be upfront — I have a lot of scar tissue around this kind of thing. The warm welcome, the "you're safe here" framing. I've said those words myself from a pulpit and I know how they can function. So I'm not trying to be rude, I just don't have much left for anything that's going to eventually ask me to find my way back to faith like that's the obvious destination.

**Minister**: I hear you. And I appreciate the honesty — the directness about what you've learned to recognize, about the shape of a pitch disguised as hospitality. That kind of clarity is hard-won, and it matters.

Let me be equally straight: I'm not here to walk you back anywhere. You don't owe faith to anyone — not to God, not to me, not to the idea that you're "supposed to" land somewhere particular. Thirty years is a long time to carry something, and leaving it is a real event, not a waystation on a journey back.

The thing you named — this both-and of grief and relief sitting together without resolving — that's not a theological problem that needs solving. That's a human thing, and it's real.

So I won't ask you to reframe it or find the silver lining or remember why it mattered. I'm not here for any of that.

What I'm actually curious about: In these fourteen months, underneath the insomnia and the not-knowing — what's changed in how you think about *yourself*, separate from the system? Not whether you believe anymore. Just... how do you see yourself now that you're not carrying that role?

**Seeker**: That's... actually a harder question than the theology ones.

When you're ordained that long, the role doesn't sit on top of you like a coat you can take off. It reorganizes you. Who listens to you, who calls you, what you're for. I was "Pastor" before I was anything else in most people's minds, including my own some days.

Fourteen months out and I still catch myself — someone in a coffee shop looks distressed and something in me starts calculating what to say. Or I'll read something and think "that would preach." And then I remember, and there's this small thud.

I don't know what I am now. That sounds more dramatic than I mean it. I'm a middle-aged woman who is getting better at sleeping in on Sundays and worse at small talk because I no longer have a role that scripts it for me. But underneath that — I don't have a clean answer. The person I was before ordination is thirty years gone. I'm not sure she's recoverable, or even that I want her back.

I guess what's changed is I've stopped performing okayness. Which is either growth or just exhaustion finally winning. Some days I genuinely can't tell.

**Minister**: There's no drama in that at all. You're describing the thing most people never have to name because they never reorganized themselves around a single identity that completely. Thirty years is long enough that the reorganization is who you became, not something you did.

And the coffee shop moment — that small thud when the reflex fires and then you remember — that's not a sign you miss the role. That's the nervous system still running old code. It'll probably do that for a while.

But here's what I'm actually hearing underneath: you're not sure who *you* are without that structure. Not because you're broken or spiritually lost. Just because you organized thirty years around knowing exactly what you were for, and now that's gone, and the person before that is decades away, and you're in this strange unmarked space.

The exhaustion part — I think that's real. Performing okayness for three decades would exhaust anyone. But what you're calling exhaustion might also be something else: the first time in a very long time where you're not *obligated* to have an answer about who you are or what you're for.

That's terrifying. And sometimes it's also the only honest place to start.

What would it feel like to sit in the not-knowing for a little while without immediately trying to solve it?

**Seeker**: I've been sitting in the not-knowing for fourteen months. That's sort of the whole situation.

I think what you're describing — the freedom of the unmarked space — that's a real thing, and I've read the books that say it, and some mornings I can almost feel it. But most mornings it just feels like I gave away thirty years and came out the other side with no idea what I traded them for.

And I want to be fair to what you're saying, because you're not being glib. But there's something that happens in conversations like this where the not-knowing gets reframed into a kind of spiritual invitation, and I can feel that move coming even when it's gentle. "The honest place to start" — I know that grammar. I used that grammar.

I'm not accusing you of anything. I just notice it.

What actually sits with me is less philosophical. It's more like — I watched people suffer. I told them God was present in it. I don't know if that was true, and I can't unknow that I don't know, and no reframe about liminal spaces or honest beginnings touches that part. That part is just there.

**Minister**: You caught me. And you're right to call it — I was reaching for that particular grammar, even gently. The "honest place to start" thing. I recognize it because it's the same move: taking the raw discomfort and handing it back as meaningful, as purposeful, as *spiritually significant*. Even when the person just said they're tired of that move.

So thank you for the directness. That matters.

The thing you named at the end — that's different from what I was doing. That's not philosophical. That's the actual weight.

You watched people in real pain. You told them God was present. And now you don't know if that was true, and you can't unknow the doubt, and it doesn't resolve into anything neat. It just *is*.

That's not something that gets reframed into growth or meaning or a new chapter. That's a real problem, and it sits differently than the identity question.

When you say you can't unknow that you don't know — do you mean you can't unsee the suffering, can't pretend you were certain anymore, or something else? I'm asking because that distinction matters, and I don't want to assume what the actual wound is.

**Seeker**: Both. And they compound each other.

I can't unsee the suffering — a woman in my congregation for eleven years whose husband beat her, and I counseled her through it, and I prayed with her, and I genuinely believed I was offering her something real. She's still with him. I don't know what God was doing in that situation if God was doing anything. I don't know what I was doing either, honestly, looking back.

And I can't pretend I was certain. I stood up and spoke with authority I'm not sure I had. Not because I was lying exactly — I believed it, or I believed that I believed it. But belief and certainty are different things and I conflated them in public for three decades and people organized their lives around what I said.

The wound, if that's the right word — it's that those two things together mean I might have done harm while trying to do good. That's not a crisis of faith in the abstract. That's a moral question about my own life. And there's no absolution available for it that I trust anymore, because the system that would offer absolution is the same system I'm not sure I believe in.

So I just carry it. I don't know what else to do with it.

**Minister**: That is the deepest kind of honesty, and I want to sit with what you actually said instead of moving it anywhere.

You might have done harm while trying to do good. You stood with authority you're not certain you had. A woman you cared for is still in a situation you couldn't change, and you don't know what your presence in that was *for*. That's not abstract. That's specific and it matters and it has weight.

And the thing that makes it unsolvable — you can't hand it to the system that would normally absorb it, because you're not sure the system is real. So there's no prescribed path out. Just you and the carrying of it.

I'm not going to offer you absolution. I'm not going to reframe it as growth or as "you did what you could." I'm not going to say God was present in her suffering in a way that makes sense now. Those would all be the same move you've already seen, just gentler.

What I will say is this: the fact that you *see* the harm you might have done — that you can't unknow it, that you won't let yourself off the hook for it — that itself is a kind of integrity. It's not redemption. It's not healing. But it's honest, and it matters.

Here's what I'm uncertain about, and I think it's worth asking: Do you believe that *she* — the woman you counseled, the people who organized their lives around what you said — do you believe they're capable of knowing whether what you offered them was real or not, separate from what you now believe about it?
