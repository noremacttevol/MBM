Both, I think. But if I'm honest — mostly how God saw me. I was taught that who I am was an offense. Not just a struggle, an *offense*. So I wasn't just praying to be straight, I was praying to stop being disgusting. That's what I was actually doing on my knees at seventeen.

Real people with real authority taught you that you were disgusting. They did that *in the name of* a God they said was good. And whether or not their theology was technically "distorted," the damage is real — it happened in your body, in your relationship with yourself, in years of your actual life. That's not erased by saying "well, that wasn't the real God." It doesn't undo anything.

You're not asking me to fix that or reframe it. You're asking me to be honest about it. So: yes. That happened. It was wrong. And the people who did it were speaking from a tradition that many others believed sincerely — which makes it worse in some ways, not better, because it was systemic.

I can't undo fifteen years or the damage in those years. And I'm not going to insult you by trying to spiritually repackage it.

Here's what I *can* say honestly: you survived it. You got to the other side of it. You don't believe you're disgusting anymore — and that's not because you're in denial about faith, it's because you saw the truth about yourself that prayer never gave you.

What made the difference? What actually changed your mind about who you are?

**Seeker**: That's... actually a good question. And you didn't flinch when I pushed back, so fine.

What changed it wasn't anything religious. It was people. Specifically it was a friend who knew everything about where I'd come from and just — didn't treat me like a project. Didn't have an agenda for me. Just showed up and kept showing up. First person in my life who made me feel like my existence wasn't a problem to be solved.

And therapy. A good therapist who helped me see that I'd internalized a voice that wasn't mine. That the disgust I felt wasn't some deep spiritual truth about me, it was something that had been installed. That took a long time to believe but eventually I did.

The prayer didn't change me. The people did.

And I notice that's a kind of irony I'm still sitting with — because the thing the church was supposed to provide, genuine unconditional presence, I found everywhere except there.

I'm not trying to make a theological argument. I'm just telling you what actually happened.

**Minister**: You're not making a theological argument — you're telling me the truth. And there's something important in that, so let me sit with it rather than rush past it.

A friend who showed up without an agenda. A therapist who helped you hear your own voice under the installed one. That's what actually reached you. Not doctrine, not prayer, not a reframed theology — *presence*. Someone who treated your existence as *not a problem*.

And yes, that's an irony you're still sitting with, and you should. The church taught you that God loved you unconditionally while simultaneously treating your existence as something to fix. The people outside the church just... loved you. Without the theology.

Here's what I'm noticing, and I say this gently: you found what actually works — what actually changes a person — and it has nothing to do with belief systems. It's presence. Dignity. Being known and chosen anyway. Your friend didn't need you to believe anything to show up for you. Your therapist didn't need you to accept a doctrine. They just saw you as real, and that was enough to help you see yourself that way.

I want to ask you something, and I genuinely don't know your answer: when you look at what actually saved you — that unconditional presence, that refusal to treat you as a project — do you think that's a *Christian* thing, even if the church failed to be it? Or does naming it as Christian feel like it's reclaiming something that hurt you, and you're not interested in that reclamation?

I'm asking because I actually want to know where you stand on that, not because I'm angling you somewhere.
