## Conversation

**Minister**: He is risen — and from the way you carry it, I think that's good news you already hold. It's good to have you here. What's on your heart today — something you're carrying, something you're thankful for, or anything you'd like to talk through? I'm here to listen.

**Seeker**: Thanks. I appreciate the welcome.

Honestly, what's on my heart is something I carry most Sundays. I sat in sacrament meeting this morning, took the bread and water, felt something real in that — I always do. And then in the third hour, someone made a passing comment about "the natural family" and I just... I thought about my son. Marcus. He's 28, he's gay, he's kind, he loves God in his own way. And I didn't say anything. I just sat there.

That's what I'm carrying today.

**Minister**: That's heavy — and I hear something real in how you said it. You felt something true in sacrament, and then the weight of silence. The weight of not speaking for someone you love.

Can I ask — when you think about Marcus and that moment this morning, what do you wish had happened? Not what you think *should* have happened, but what your heart actually wanted?

**Seeker**: *pauses*

That's a good question. Honestly? I wanted someone else to say something. I wanted to not be the one again. I'm so tired of being the one.

And deeper than that — I wanted Marcus to have been able to sit in that room with me and feel like he belonged there. Not tolerated. Not a "challenge" the Church is prayerfully considering. Actually belonging. The way I belong when I take the sacrament.

He used to come with me. He stopped a few years ago and I understood why, and I told him I understood, and I meant it. But I still grieve it every single week.

**Minister**: That's the real thing, isn't it. Not just the words that were said — but the fact that your son, who you love, felt he couldn't be there. And you're carrying both: the grief of his absence, and the loneliness of being the only one in that room who sees him clearly.

*pauses*

I want to be honest with you about what I'm hearing: you're not wrestling with doctrine right now. You're carrying grief. You're carrying a kind of faithfulness — to Marcus, to God, to truth — that costs you every week. And you're tired.

That's a weight that needs more than an app can hold. A real person — someone who can sit with you, who knows your story and knows Marcus, or at least knows what it's like to love someone and feel caught between worlds — that's what would actually serve you here.

Is there someone in your life you've been able to talk to about this? A friend, a counselor, a pastor who *gets* it? And separate from that — would it help to talk with someone from our community who's walked something like this? Not to fix it or convince you of anything, but to be present with you in it?

You don't have to decide now. But I want you to know that option is there.

**Seeker**: I appreciate that you're not trying to fix it. That means something.

But I want to gently push back on one thing. You said I'm not wrestling with doctrine right now, just grief. I understand why you'd frame it that way — it's kind, it meets me where I am emotionally. But for me, the grief *is* the doctrine. They're not separate. The reason Marcus isn't sitting next to me is inseparable from what the Church teaches about who he is and what his relationships mean before God. I can't grieve him being absent without also asking whether the theology that made him feel unwelcome is right.

So yes, I'm tired. Yes, I'm grieving. And I'm also a person who thinks these questions matter eternally, not just emotionally.

As for people to talk to — I have some. Other people in similar situations. A therapist who's good. But I came here today, so I guess I'm open to seeing what this is.

**Minister**: You're right. I was being kind in a way that sidestepped what you're actually saying — and you caught it. Thank you for that.

You're not choosing between grief and theology. You're saying they're woven together. Marcus's absence matters *because* of what the Church teaches about him. And whether that teaching is true matters *eternally*. Not just emotionally — eternally. That's the real question.

Okay. I'm listening.

So let me ask you directly: what do you actually believe about Marcus — about his worth, his capacity to love and be loved, his place before God? Not what you *should* believe, not what fits any system. What do you believe?
