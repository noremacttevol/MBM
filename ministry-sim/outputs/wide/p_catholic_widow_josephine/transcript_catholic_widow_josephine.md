The Church teaches that Mary and the saints are alive in Christ, not gone. So when I ask her to intercede for Bernard, I'm not speaking into empty air. We're all still part of the same body, the living and the dead. That's what I believe. That's what keeps the rosary from feeling like... just talking to myself.

**Minister**: You're describing something most people are afraid to name — that direct, quiet knowing that *someone is there*. Not sentiment. Not the comfort of ritual alone. An actual presence.

And you've thought about it carefully enough to know the difference between the two: the reaching that sustains you when you're empty, *and* the moments when something *answers* — settles in you — in a way you can't chalk up to your own mind. That distinction matters. You're not naive about it.

The communion of saints — that the living and dead are still one body in Christ, still bound together — that's ancient. That's not a modern invention. And if that's real, then your prayers for Bernard aren't into void; they're into a real relationship that death didn't break.

Here's what I'm wondering, and you don't have to answer if it doesn't fit: On the days when the grief sits heavier, when that settling presence doesn't come — what happens in you then? Does the doubt creep in about whether he's okay, or whether Mary hears? Or is it just the sheer weight of missing him in *this* life, whether or not you feel held?

**Seeker**: That's... a good question. And an honest one.

It's mostly the missing, if I'm truthful. When the heavy days come, I don't usually find myself doubting Mary or doubting that Bernard is in God's hands. The faith holds, even when it feels dry. Dry faith is still faith — one of our priests said that once and it stayed with me.

What I miss is *him*. His voice at the kitchen table. The way he'd take my hand after Mass without saying anything. Those things aren't coming back in this life, and no amount of prayer changes that, and some mornings that's just... hard to sit with.

I suppose what I'm saying is the grief and the faith aren't really fighting each other. They're just both there. The Church has always known that sorrow and hope can live in the same heart — we see it in the psalms, we see it in Our Lady herself standing at the foot of the cross.

**Minister**: You've just said something that a lot of people spend years trying to learn, and you're living it: that grief and faith don't cancel each other out. They're not opponents. They're both true at once.

And you're right — the psalms know it. Mary knew it. The deepest places in Scripture never ask you to choose between "God is good" and "this hurts terribly." They hold both. You're standing in very old, very solid ground.

What you're missing — his hand, his voice, the small dailiness of him — that's not something prayer is supposed to fix or make you "get over." That's the real cost of loving someone in time and losing them in time. And missing him isn't a failure of faith. It's the other side of what you two had.

Some days that's easier to remember than others. Some mornings the kitchen table is just empty, and no theology changes that particular ache.

I'm wondering — and tell me if this lands or doesn't — but do you have people around you who know you're carrying this? Not to "help you move on," but just to *know* you're here, still missing him, still praying for him? The Church is meant to be a body that carries things together, and fourteen months in, sometimes people forget to ask.
