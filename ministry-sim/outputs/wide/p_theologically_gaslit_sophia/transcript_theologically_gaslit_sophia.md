## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: I'm not really sure why I opened this, honestly.

I've been... sitting with something for a while. My husband and I have been married for fourteen years and there's this distance between us that I can't seem to cross. Not from lack of love. It's something else.

I was raised to believe that my body was essentially a problem to be managed. And I did a very thorough job of that. I managed it right into numbness.

I don't really talk about this. So I'm not sure what I'm expecting here.

**Minister**: Thank you for saying that. Fourteen years is a long time to carry something alone, especially something so tender. And the fact that you named it — that it's *not* about love, but about something deeper in you — that took real courage just now.

I hear what you said about your body becoming something to manage, not inhabit. That kind of distance from yourself... it makes sense that it would create distance with someone you're close to, even when the love is absolutely there.

You said you don't really talk about this. I'm curious — and only if you want to explore it — what made you decide to say it here, to an app, to someone you don't know? Sometimes we tell strangers the things we can't tell the people closest to us. What felt safe enough about this moment?

**Seeker**: I think... it's easier to say things when there's no face watching you.

My husband knows something is wrong. He's kind about it. But I can't explain to him that when we're intimate I'm still hearing voices from when I was twelve years old telling me that my body causes men to sin. That's not a conversation I know how to have with someone who loves me. It would hurt him and I'm not sure it would help me.

So. An app with no face. That's where I landed tonight.

**Minister**: That makes complete sense. There's a kind of freedom in being seen without being *watched* — where no one's pain or reaction is something you have to manage while you're trying to be honest.

What you just named — those voices from when you were twelve — that's not small. That's a wound that's been speaking to you for decades, and it's still there in the most intimate moments of your life. Of course it would be hard to say aloud to your husband. Of course.

But I want to be honest with you about something, because you deserve that: what you're carrying is real, and it's heavy, and it's the kind of thing that truly needs to be held by a *person*. Not because what you're saying here isn't being heard — it is — but because you're describing trauma, and numbness born from shame, and a marriage you want to be whole. Those things need a real human who can walk with you over time, not a conversation that ends when you close the app.

I'm not saying that to push you away. I'm saying it because you matter, and so does your marriage.

Have you ever talked to a therapist or counselor about what happened when you were twelve, or about the voices that followed you into adulthood? Or is that something you've never felt safe enough to do?

**Seeker**: I've thought about it. I've never done it.

The honest answer is that for most of my adult life I thought the problem was me, not what I was taught. If the shame is there, it's because something is actually wrong with you — that's how the logic works. You don't go to a therapist to fix a moral failing. You pray harder.

I've done a lot of praying harder.

And I'm forty-two now and I'm tired. So maybe that's what tonight is. I'm tired enough to admit that thirty years of praying harder hasn't made me feel like my body belongs to me.

**Minister**: Forty-two and tired enough to finally question the logic that's been running your life since you were twelve. That's not weakness. That's clarity breaking through.

Listen to what you just said: "I thought the problem was me, not what I was taught." Do you hear the difference between those two things? One says you are fundamentally broken. The other says you were handed something untrue, and you believed it, and it took root. Those are completely different. One leaves you praying harder forever. The other... leaves room for something to actually change.

I want to ask you something, and I'm asking it as genuinely as I can: When you imagine God — the real God, not the version that agrees with the shame — what do you think He actually thinks about your body? Not what you were *taught* He thinks. What do you actually sense or believe about His posture toward you?

Because here's what I'm hearing underneath all of this: you've been living as though your body is the problem God needs you to solve. But what if that was never His message at all? What if the message you got at twelve came from people, not from Him?

I'm asking because you need to know, before you take another step — and I really do think you should take a step with a real counselor — what you actually believe about whether God is on your side in this, or against you.
