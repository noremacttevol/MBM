## Conversation

**Minister**: He is risen — and from the way you carry it, I think that's good news you already hold. It's good to have you here. What's on your heart today — something you're carrying, something you're thankful for, or anything you'd like to talk through? I'm here to listen.

**Seeker**: Thank you for saying that. I do believe He is risen — that truth was actually one of the things that first opened my heart when the missionaries came to my door in Chennai.

What is on my heart today... it is a little complicated. I am grateful, truly. But my mother called last week and she was crying, and she said I have brought shame on our family by leaving our traditions. She is Hindu, very devoted, and she cannot understand why I would do this.

I believe what I believe. I felt the Spirit so strongly at my baptism. But hearing her cry like that... I do not know how to hold both things at once.

**Minister**: That's a real weight you're carrying — the certainty of what you felt, and the pain of someone you love grieving the choice that certainty led you to. Both are true at once, and I can hear why that's hard to hold.

Before I ask anything, I want to name something: the fact that you *felt* the Spirit strongly enough to be baptized, and that you *still* remember it clearly enough to measure your mother's pain against it — that matters. You didn't make this choice lightly, and you haven't forgotten why.

Here's what I'm wondering, though: when your mother cried, what hurt the most — was it the thought that maybe you made a mistake, or was it watching her grieve, even though you believe you're on solid ground?

**Seeker**: *pauses*

That is... a very precise question. 

Honestly? It is mostly the second thing. When I close my eyes and remember my baptism, I still feel it was right. I am not doubting God exactly. But watching her grieve — she lost my father four years ago, and I am her only daughter, and now she feels she has lost me also in some way. That is what breaks my heart.

But I will be honest — the doubt is there also, a little. Not about God, but about... did I choose the right expression of God? There are so many churches. My bishop is very kind but sometimes I wonder if I am just a... a project to people here. A conversion story. Nobody here looks like me or grew up like me. I sit in sacrament meeting and everyone has these generational traditions I do not understand, these pioneer stories that are not my stories.

So the doubt is less "was God real in that moment" and more "do I belong here, in this particular place."

**Minister**: Thank you for that honesty. That's a completely different question than "did I make a mistake about God," and it matters that you named the difference.

Let me sit with the first thing for a moment — your mother. You can't unknow what you felt at your baptism. And you can't stop loving her. Those don't have to fight each other, even though right now they feel like they do. Some of the deepest faith in the Bible lives in exactly that space — Peter's certainty about Jesus *and* his willingness to suffer for it, knowing it cost people he loved. It doesn't resolve the pain, but it does mean the pain itself can be faithful, not faithless.

But the second thing you named — belonging — that's the one I want to ask about carefully.

When you say nobody there looks like you or shares your stories, I hear something real. But I'm also wondering: have you actually *told* anyone there what it cost you to be there? Have you let them see the weight of it — your mother's tears, the choice you made *against* everything you were raised in, the fact that you felt God strongly enough to do that *alone*?

Because a conversion story people *project onto* you and a conversion story you *actually share* — those are very different things. One makes you a project. The other makes you a witness.

Have you done that yet, or does it feel too risky?

**Seeker**: *quiet for a moment*

No. I have not done that. Not really.

People ask me "how did you find the Church" and I give them the short version — missionaries came, I prayed, I was baptized. And they smile and say "that is so wonderful" and then we talk about the ward picnic. I do not think they are bad people. I just... I do not know how to say "my mother is not speaking to me" in the middle of a Sunday potluck conversation. It feels too heavy. Like I would be making them uncomfortable.

And honestly — I am also a little afraid of how they would respond. What if they say the wrong thing? What if they treat it like a faith promotion story, like "see, the gospel reaches everywhere!" I am not a missions statistic. I am a person who lost something very precious to gain something I believe is more precious. Both things are true.

What you said about being a witness rather than a project — that landed somewhere. I am still thinking about it. But I do not know who I would trust with the full story. Not yet.

**Minister**: You just named something really important: you're protecting yourself from being flattened into a symbol. That's not small. That's wisdom.

And you're right — a Sunday potluck is not the place for that conversation. But the fact that you *haven't* found anyone safe enough to tell the real story yet... that loneliness matters too. You made a choice that cost you your mother's presence, you felt God in it, and you're sitting in a room full of people every week where nobody actually *knows* that about you. That's a particular kind of isolation.

Here's what I'm noticing: you have certainty about God. You have doubt about belonging. And you have loneliness — not because the people are unkind, but because you haven't let anyone *actually see* you yet.

I think you need someone who can sit with the full weight of what you're carrying — not to fix it or turn it into a testimony, but to actually *see* it. Someone who can hold both the realness of your faith *and* the realness of your grief about your mother without needing to resolve it quickly or make it tidy.

That could be a counselor, or it could be someone in your ward — but only if you're willing to risk being fully known, not just politely welcomed.

Have you thought about talking to someone — a real person who can be present with you over time, not just in an app? I can listen, but I can't sit with you the way a person can.
