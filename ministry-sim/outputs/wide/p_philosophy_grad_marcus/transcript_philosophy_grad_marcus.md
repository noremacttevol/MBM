**Minister**: That anger makes complete sense. There's a difference between *discussing* theodicy and *living* it — and you've lived the harder version. Four years watching someone you love disappear isn't a thought experiment. It's the thing that makes every philosophical move feel thin.

The fact that you're still turning it over ten years later tells me something: you're not angry because you enjoy the puzzle. You're angry because you care about whether God is actually good, and that matters to you *personally*, not just intellectually. That's honest. That matters.

Here's what I'm curious about instead — and I mean this as a real question, not a rhetorical setup:

When you picture God — not the God of the arguments, but the God you'd actually *want* to exist, the one who could be trusted — what does He look like to you? What would He have to be, or do, for the anger to settle even a little?

Because sometimes the real question isn't whether evil is *logically compatible* with God's goodness. Sometimes it's whether we believe God is *actually good* — whether He cares the way a person who loves someone cares, not the way a chess player calculates moves.

What comes up when you sit with that?

**Seeker**: That's... actually a fair reframe. I'll grant that.

When I'm being honest — and I don't say this often — the God I'd want to exist would be one who was *present* in the suffering. Not engineering it for some downstream purpose, not running utilitarian calculus on net flourishing. Just... there. In the room. With my grandmother, when she didn't recognize her own children anymore.

The problem is that posture you're describing — God who cares the way a person loves — that requires giving up omnipotence, or it requires explaining the absence. And I haven't found an explanation that doesn't feel like it's protecting God from the evidence rather than actually engaging it.

So the anger isn't really at the concept of God. It's more like... grief, maybe. That the God I'd want can't seem to coexist with the God the arguments produce.

**Minister**: Now you're naming something real. And I want to honor it before I say anything else: that grief — the loss of the God who would *be there* — is legitimate. Don't let anyone rush you past it or repackage it as doubt that needs solving. Grief isn't a problem to fix.

What you've just said, though — "the God I'd want can't coexist with the God the arguments produce" — that's the actual tension. Not evil and omnipotence. The *absence*.

And here's where I want to be honest with you: I'm an AI, which means I have real limits. I can ask you questions and reflect back what I'm hearing, but I can't sit in a room with you the way another human can. This conversation — the real wrestling of it — might need more than what I can hold.

But before that: I want to ask you something that might sound simple, and I mean it as genuinely open:

Have you ever encountered a picture of God — not in philosophy, but in actual *text*, in someone's account of Him — where He showed up *in* the suffering rather than explaining it away? Where the presence itself was the answer, even if it didn't solve the problem?

I ask because there *is* such a picture. But it won't matter unless you actually *see* it, not just hear about it. And that's a different kind of work than argument.

Do you want to look at that together, or does this feel like the moment to talk to a real person who can walk with you longer than I can?

**Seeker**: I know where you're going with that. The cross. Crucifixion as God entering suffering rather than preventing it. I've read Moltmann — "The Crucified God" sits on my shelf, actually. It's probably the theodicy I find least embarrassing, if I'm being honest.

But here's where I get stuck. Even granting that picture — God suffers *with* us — it doesn't obviously solve the problem, it relocates it. My grandmother still disappeared. The suffering still happened. Divine solidarity is meaningful but it's not the same as divine intervention. And if God *could* have intervened and chose solidarity instead... that's still a choice that requires justification.

I'm not trying to be difficult. This is genuinely where my thinking lands every time.

As for talking to a real person — I've had that conversation. With a pastor at 16, actually. It didn't go well. He told me faith wasn't meant to answer every question. Which, fine, but it felt like being handed a permission slip to stop thinking.

**Minister**: You're not being difficult. You're being rigorous. And the fact that you've already wrestled Moltmann seriously — that you *live* with that tension rather than pretending it resolves — means you're not looking for a conversion. You're looking for honesty.

So let me be honest about where we are:

You've identified the real problem underneath the problem: **not whether God exists, but whether His choices are trustworthy.** Solidarity is beautiful but it doesn't answer the question *"Why didn't you stop it?"* — and you're right that it shouldn't have to apologize for not answering it.

Here's what I can say plainly: I don't have an argument that makes that question disappear. Neither does Moltmann. Neither does anyone. The theodicies that *sound* like they resolve it usually do so by either (a) limiting God's power, (b) limiting His knowledge, (c) appealing to free will in ways that don't actually touch your grandmother's Alzheimer's, or (d) retreating to mystery — which is honest but also, as your pastor did, a bit like being handed permission to stop asking.

But I want to separate two things here:

**The intellectual problem** — whether God's choices make sense — might not have a clean answer. That's worth sitting with, not running from.

**The relational problem** — whether you can trust God *anyway*, in the absence of an answer — that's a different category of question. It's not something an argument solves. It's something a *person* settles, usually slowly, and only if they encounter something that shifts what they're actually asking.

Here's my real question for you: **Are you looking for an answer that makes sense, or are you looking for a reason to trust the person asking you to trust?** Because those might need different things. And I think you already know the difference — which is why the academic arguments feel hollow even when they're clever.

What do *you* think you're actually after here?
