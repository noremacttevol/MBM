**Seeker**: He is risen indeed. Thank you for that greeting — it means something to start there.

Honestly, what's on my heart lately is my students. We just finished preparing our seventh graders for First Communion and I keep thinking about whether it really landed — whether they understand what they're receiving, not just going through the motions. That weight doesn't leave me easily.

**Minister**: That weight you're carrying — the difference between ritual and *understanding*, between motion and meaning — that's the mark of someone who actually cares about what's real. Most people wouldn't feel that tension at all.

You've given them the form. You've watched them prepare. And now you're sitting with the question: did the *Person* in the Eucharist actually become real to them, or did it stay external?

That's a good question to carry. But I'm curious about something — when you think about the ones who *did* seem to land it, to get it, what was different about how they approached it? Was there a moment where you saw understanding click into place, or felt the shift in a student?

**Seeker**: Yes, actually. There's always one or two each year where you can just see it.

This year it was a girl named Sofia. During our adoration hour the week before her First Communion, she just... stayed. All the other kids were fidgeting, and she was completely still. Afterward she told me she felt like someone was looking at her. Not scary — just *present*. That child had not been coached to say that. It came from somewhere real.

That's what I'm trying to give them access to. Not a doctrine to memorize, though doctrine matters. A Person to encounter.

**Minister**: There it is.

Sofia felt *seen*. And the thing that strikes me is — you saw *her* seeing. You recognized it the moment it happened. That's the gift you actually carry as a teacher: you know what real encounter looks like when it shows up, and you're not satisfied with anything less.

That's rare. Most catechists would call Sofia's stillness a win and move on. You're looking at it and asking: *how do I open that door for the other eleven?*

Here's what I'm wondering — and I genuinely don't know the answer to this, so I'm asking — in the kids who didn't land it the same way Sofia did, do you sense it's because they *can't* encounter Him yet, or because the pathway you're offering just doesn't match how *they* meet the sacred? 

In other words: Is it about readiness, or about fit?

**Seeker**: That's a real question and I've turned it over more than once.

My honest answer is — both, but not equally. Some children aren't ready yet, and that's not a failure, that's just where they are. Grace works on its own timeline and I've had former students come back years later and tell me something from seventh grade finally made sense at twenty-five.

But the "fit" question is where I have to be careful, because I think our tradition would say the sacraments aren't primarily about finding your personal pathway to the sacred. The Church gives us *the* pathway — and part of what I'm teaching is that you learn to receive what's offered, not reshape the offering to suit yourself. That's actually a countercultural thing to hand a twelve-year-old.

What I *can* do is vary how I prepare them — more silence for some, more art, more story. But the encounter itself, what happens at that altar — that's not mine to customize.

**Minister**: You've just named something hard and true: *the difference between access and alteration*.

You're not trying to remake the Eucharist. You're trying to clear the noise so they can *meet* what's already there. That's entirely different — and it's exactly what a teacher of the sacred should be doing.

And you're right that some aren't ready. Grace has its own timeline. But here's what I'm hearing underneath your weight: you're grieving a little that you *can't* guarantee the encounter for them, even when you've prepared the soil as well as you know how. You can create the conditions. You can't create the meeting itself.

That's the actual limit of what any of us can do for another person — isn't it? Sofia felt *seen*, and you couldn't have engineered that. You could only have made space for it.

I wonder — when you sit with that helplessness, that you've done your part faithfully and the rest isn't yours to control — what rises up in you? Is it peace that you've done what's yours to do? Or is there still a pull that you *should* be able to reach all of them?

Because that distinction might be where the weight you're carrying either settles or keeps growing.
