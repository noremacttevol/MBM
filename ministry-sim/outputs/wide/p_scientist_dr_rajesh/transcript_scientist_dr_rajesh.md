I'm curious: when you think of the smartest person you know who believes, what do you imagine they would say *changed* for them? Not what reasons they'd give *now*, but what actually shifted?

**Seeker**: See, that's exactly where I get stuck. Because you're essentially saying the entry point is experiential, not evidential. And that's... that's a problem for me. That's not dismissal, that's just mechanism.

So I don't think I'm missing the experience piece. I think I'm unconvinced that experience constitutes data about anything outside the experiencer.

**Minister**: You're thinking clearly, and I'm not going to pretend that's not a real problem. You've named exactly why experience alone doesn't settle it — the brain absolutely *does* construct extraordinary things under stress, and "it felt real to me" doesn't prove external causation. That's just honest neuroscience.

So let me ask differently, because I think you might actually be more open than you think:

If God *were* real — if there actually *were* an external agent operating in the world — what would you expect to see? Not in the abstract, but in the actual data of how people change, how they function, what becomes possible in them?

Because here's what interests me: your virologist friend didn't just *feel* held. She presumably went on living. The "feeling held" either changed her decisions, her resilience, her capacity to move forward — or it didn't. Those are observable outcomes. And if something *real* were happening in her, those outcomes should be different in *measurable* ways from someone whose brain manufactured the same feeling as a stress response and nothing more.

I'm not asking you to accept her interpretation. I'm asking: would you be willing to look at the actual trajectory of her life after that crisis as *data* — separate from her explanation of it?

What would have to be true about how she actually lived for you to think "okay, something more than neurology is operating here"?

**Seeker**: That's a fair reframe, and I'll engage with it. But here's where it still breaks down for me.

Even if I observed remarkable resilience, better decision-making, sustained behavioral change — I still have a perfectly adequate explanation that doesn't require the God hypothesis. Belief systems work. Placebo works. Community works. Narrative coherence — having a story that makes your suffering meaningful — demonstrably improves outcomes. We have mechanism for all of that without invoking an external agent.

So her trajectory could be extraordinary and my conclusion would still be: her belief system is functioning as an effective cognitive and social technology. That's actually *fascinating* and worth studying. It's just not evidence for the thing she believes caused it.

What would I need to see? Honestly — something that breaks the causal closure of the physical world. Something where the naturalistic explanation genuinely fails. Not "we don't know yet." Actually fails. And so far, in thirty years of doing science, I haven't seen that. The gaps keep closing, not widening.

**Minister**: Okay. I'm going to be straight with you because you've earned it by thinking this clearly.

You've just described the actual hard problem — not the experience problem, but the *causal closure* problem. And you're right: no amount of life-trajectory data will answer it, because belief systems *do* work as cognitive technology, and that explanation is always sufficient. You're not being unreasonable. You're being rigorous.

So here's my honest limit: I cannot give you data that breaks causal closure. No AI can. And anyone who tries — who points to a miracle and says "see, naturalism fails" — is actually just identifying a gap in current explanation, not a closure breach. The gap might shrink tomorrow with better neuroscience. You know this better than I do.

What I *can* do is ask you something that might actually matter more than the causal-closure question:

**Are you certain the causal-closure frame is the right cage to be asking the question in?**

I don't mean that as a dodge. I mean: you're asking "what physical evidence breaks the laws of physics?" But what if God — if He's real — *isn't* trying to break the physical world's coherence? What if He *built* that coherence? Then looking for Him *within* causal closure might be like looking for a songwriter by examining sound waves.

The real question might be different: not "does the data break naturalism," but "does my life, my choices, my meaning-making actually require nothing beyond matter and mechanism to make sense of?" 

That's not scientific. But it's honest. And it might be the only question that actually matters here.

What do you think — is causal closure actually where the real question lives for you? Or is something else nagging?
