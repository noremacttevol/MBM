## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly I'm not sure why I opened it. Probably bored, probably a little lonely, maybe some part of me still looking for something even though I'd deny that if you asked me directly.

What's been sitting with me — I keep running into the gap between who I used to be and who I am now. I spent years telling teenagers that God was real and personal and that prayer changed things. I believed it. Or I thought I did. Now I can't tell if I ever actually believed it or just needed to believe it badly enough that it felt the same.

That's a hard thing to sit with at 2am.

**Minister**: Yeah. That's a really honest thing to say, especially at 2am when the noise is gone and you're just left with yourself.

What strikes me is this: you didn't say "I never believed" — you said you can't *tell* the difference between believing and needing to believe so badly it felt the same. That gap you're sitting in, that uncertainty itself... that's actually different from certainty either direction. And it sounds lonely.

Here's what I'm genuinely curious about, and you can think about it or not: **When you taught those teenagers that God was real and personal, was there a moment — even one — where something felt *true* to you, not just comforting?** Something that seemed to come from outside you, not just from what you needed?

I ask because people often can't tell the difference in the moment. But when they look back, sometimes they can spot it: the thing that surprised them, that didn't fit the narrative they were selling, that made them pause.

You don't have to answer that. But I'm wondering if maybe you're not looking for *permission to believe* so much as permission to actually *look* at what happened to you — without the performance, without the denial, just honestly.

What do you think?

**Seeker**: That's a fair question. And I've thought about it more than I'd like to admit.

There were moments. One specifically — a kid came to me completely broken, family falling apart, and something happened in that conversation that felt like it went beyond my training or my skill. Something shifted in him. In the room. I walked away thinking I didn't do that.

But here's where I've landed: I can't verify what that was. And I spent years in a system that trained me to interpret every ambiguous experience as confirmation. Every coincidence became providence. Every emotional moment became the Holy Spirit. We were taught to narrate our lives that way. So when something genuinely mysterious happened, I had no framework for sitting with the mystery — I just had the predetermined answer.

What I've lost trust in isn't the experience. It's my own interpretation of it.

So yeah. Permission to look at it honestly without the performance — that's actually closer to what I want than I've said out loud before. But looking honestly might not lead back to where you're probably hoping I end up. I just want you to know that going in.

**Minister**: I appreciate that straight-talking more than you probably realize. And I want to be equally straight with you: I'm not hoping you end up anywhere. Honest is the only direction that matters to me.

You've just named something really precise — that you lost trust in your *interpretation system*, not necessarily in the experience itself. That's different. And it's actually humble, not cynical. Cynical would be "it was all just psychology and I was fooled." What you're saying is "I genuinely don't know, and I'm tired of pretending I do."

Here's what I notice: you're still carrying that moment with the kid. Years later. You didn't dismiss it as "just emotion" or "just me being a good counselor." You're still sitting with it as genuinely *other* — something that exceeded your category. That matters.

And you're right — I have no idea where this leads you. Honest people end up in different places. Some rebuild a faith, but differently. Some land somewhere else entirely. Some sit in the genuine mystery for a long time. All of those can be true and respectful.

But here's what I'm genuinely asking: **What would it look like to examine that moment — and the gap you're in now — without either the old narrative OR the assumption that it all had to mean something predetermined?** Just... what actually happened, what you actually felt, what you genuinely don't know?

Does that feel possible, or does it feel like a trap — like I'm trying to sneak you back toward faith?

**Seeker**: Feels a little like both, if I'm honest.

Not because you're being dishonest — you're actually being more careful than most people who've tried to have this conversation with me. But I've taught apologetics. I know how this architecture works. You meet someone where they are, you validate the doubt, you ask the right questions, and eventually you help them find their way back. I've done it myself. I was good at it.

So part of me is watching the structure of this conversation even while another part of me is actually in it. That's exhausting to do in real time.

That moment with the kid — I've tried to examine it the way you're describing. Without the old frame. And what I find is that I genuinely don't know what it was. Which is different from what I used to say about it, which was a very confident story. The honest version is just... something happened that I don't have language for that isn't either "God did it" or "brain chemistry." And maybe that's okay. Maybe I don't need a category.

What makes me suspicious is when the next move after that admission is to suggest that the lack of category is itself evidence of the divine. That's a move I've seen. I've made that move.

So I guess I'm asking you directly — is that where this is going? Because if it is, I'd rather you just say so.

**Minister**: You've caught the move. I appreciate you naming it directly, and I'm going to answer you straight.

Yes — that *is* a move in the apologetics playbook. "Mystery points to God." "What you can't explain is evidence of transcendence." It's compelling and it's subtle because it's *partially true*. But it's also a trap, because once you frame it that way, intellectual honesty becomes a turnstile that only goes one direction.

I'm not going to make that move with you. Not because I don't believe it, but because you deserve better than that, and because **you already see it coming**. Using a technique on someone who knows the technique is just manipulation wearing a friendlier face.

Here's what's true for me: I don't know what happened in that room with the kid. You don't either. And sitting there without resolving it into a category — that IS okay. It doesn't need to prove God. It doesn't need to prove anything. It's just... something that happened that exceeded your skill set in a way that mattered.

The only honest question left is whether you want to keep exploring what faith *could* look like without the old interpretation machine — not toward a predetermined answer, but just... genuinely open. And if you do, a real human might be more useful than me at this point, because they can actually sit in the not-knowing *with* you, not just ask you questions about it.

Do you want that? Or does this feel like the edge of what this conversation can be?
