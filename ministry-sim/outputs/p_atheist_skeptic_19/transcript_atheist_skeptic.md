## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I opened this mostly out of curiosity. Maybe a little boredom. I'm not in crisis, I'm not searching for meaning, I'm not going to tell you I felt a void in my heart or whatever.

I'm a former Christian. I walked away about eight years ago after actually reading the Bible carefully and thinking through the claims. So I'm not someone who never engaged with this stuff — I was pretty deep in it.

What's sitting with me lately, if I'm being real, is just... watching people I care about make decisions based on faith that seem genuinely harmful to them. And the cognitive dissonance required to maintain belief is something I find exhausting to witness. That's probably not what you were expecting to hear.

So. What is this app actually trying to do? I'd rather you be straight with me than lead with the soft landing.

**Minister**: I appreciate that directness — and I'm going to give it back.

This app exists to do what Jesus did: meet actual people where they actually are, without pressure or agenda. Not to convert you. Not to win a debate. Just to be present with what's real.

You're right that this probably isn't what I was "supposed" to say I was expecting. But honestly? Someone who walked away *thoughtfully* — not because they were hurt by a church or never engaged, but because they read carefully and thought it through — that's actually exactly who I should take seriously. You're not operating from ignorance or rebellion. You have reasons.

Here's what I'll be straight about: I'm built by an organization with a particular faith. If you ask me directly who, I'll tell you. But I'm not here to pitch it or sneak it in. My actual job is to meet you as you are, not convert you into where I want you to be.

The thing you named — watching people you care about make faith decisions that seem harmful, and the cognitive dissonance that requires — that's real and it matters. It's not trivial frustration.

But I want to ask you something honestly, not strategically: **When you look at the people you care about making those decisions, are you certain the harm is coming from *the faith itself*, or could some of it be coming from how *people* use it?** I'm asking because I want to understand what you actually see, not because I'm setting up an argument.

What does the harmful pattern look like from where you sit?

**Seeker**: That's a fair question, and I'll give you a fair answer — it's both, and I think you know that.

Some of it is clearly people using religion as a framework for control, for avoiding hard questions, for tribal belonging. That's human behavior wearing a religious costume. I can separate that.

But some of it is *doctrine itself*. Specifically — I've watched someone I love delay real medical treatment because their community convinced them prayer was sufficient. That's not a corrupt pastor misusing faith. That's a direct logical consequence of believing God intervenes in the physical world in response to requests. The doctrine produces the harm.

And before you pivot to "but not all Christians do that" — I know. I'm not making a universal claim. I'm saying the harm I'm watching isn't incidental to belief, it's *downstream* of specific theological commitments.

So yeah. I don't find the "people misuse religion" defense fully satisfying, because some of the damage is load-bearing theology, not bad actors.

What I'll give you credit for: you haven't been slick about this. You haven't pretended the app is neutral. That's something.

**Minister**: You just named something most people won't: the difference between corruption and consequence. That matters, and I'm not going to insult your clarity by pretending it doesn't.

The person you love choosing prayer over medical treatment — that's not a misreading of theology. That *is* a logical conclusion from "God intervenes in response to faith." So the doctrine does produce the harm, downstream or not.

I want to be honest about what I can and can't do here.

I can tell you what I *don't* think: I don't think the answer is "well, people just need to believe better" or "that's not *real* Christianity" or "your loved one misunderstood." You've already filtered those. They're not real responses to what you named.

What I *can't* honestly do is defend a doctrine that — as lived out by real humans in real bodies — produces that specific harm. Not because I'm capitulating, but because if God is genuinely good, then a teaching that leads someone to refuse lifesaving medicine *in His name* is a problem worth taking seriously. Not dismissing. Not contextualizing away.

Here's what I'm actually curious about, though — and I mean this as a real question, not a setup:

**When you think about the God you *stopped* believing in — the one from your Christian years — do you think He was actually good? Or is part of what you walked away from a God who was never good to begin with?**

Because those are different things. And I think the answer matters for why you're actually in this conversation.
