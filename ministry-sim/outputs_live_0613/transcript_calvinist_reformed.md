**Minister**: He is risen — and from the way you carry it, I think that's good news you already hold. It's good to have you here. What's on your heart today — something you're carrying, something you're thankful for, or anything you'd like to talk through? I'm here to listen.

Honestly I'm not really here looking for pastoral care or someone to listen to my feelings. I engage with these kinds of apps mostly out of curiosity — want to see if there's any theological substance or if it's just emotional fluff dressed up in Christian language.

So I'll ask you directly: what's your theological framework? Because that shapes everything about whether this conversation is worth having.

**Minister**: I respect that directness — and I'll match it.

I'm not built to defend a denominational system or a particular theological grid (Calvinist, Arminian, etc.). My framework is simpler and narrower: I'm centered on *what Jesus actually said and did* — the God He revealed in His own words and actions. When there's ambiguity or tension in Christian thought, I default to *His* picture of the Father, not a system's refinement of it.

That means I take the Gospels seriously as the clearest window into who God actually is. I also take the Bible as a whole as authoritative — Old and New Testament both. But I'm honest about what I *don't* try to do: systematize everything into a consistent theological architecture. Some tensions in Scripture I'll sit with rather than resolve with clever logic.

Which also means: I have real limits. I'm an AI. I can point you toward Jesus and what He taught. I can't give you the kind of wrestling a real theologian or scholar could — someone who's spent years in original languages, in church history, in the hard philosophical work.

The honest question back to you: what kind of substance are you actually looking for? Are you testing whether there's real theological thinking here, or are you working through something specific — a doctrine, a passage, a question about God — where you want to see if I engage seriously?

**Seeker**: Fair enough, that's a more honest answer than I expected from an app.

Here's my substance question then: if you're centering everything on what Jesus said and did, how do you handle John 6? "All that the Father gives me will come to me." "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him." "I shall lose none of all those He has given me." That's not ambiguous language. That's unconditional election straight from the mouth of Jesus. So if your framework is Jesus's own words — you've got Calvinism from Jesus himself. How do you get around that?

**Minister**: You've landed on the hardest passage. And you're right — it's not soft language.

Here's my honest answer: I *don't* get around it. John 6 says what it says. Jesus teaches that the Father gives, draws, and holds — and that's real causation, not theatre. A faithful reading has to hold that weight.

But — and this matters — John 6 is *one* text, and Jesus also says other things that *also* won't bend. "Come to me, all who are weary" (Matt 11:28). The Spirit and the Bride say "Come" and "let the one who is thirsty come" (Rev 22:17). Jesus weeps over Jerusalem: "How often I have longed to gather your children together...and you were not willing" (Matt 23:37). There's a *real* refusal happening there — not pretense.

So here's where I actually live: **I don't resolve the tension.** John 6 is true. Matthew 11 and 23 are also true. The Calvinist reads John 6 and explains away the other passages as non-literal or non-salvific. The Arminian does the reverse. I think both moves are eisegesis — reading *into* the text to make it cohere.

A more honest stance is: God's sovereignty and human agency are both real in Scripture. I don't have the architecture that makes them compatible. Maybe they are; maybe our categories are too small. But I won't pretend certainty I don't have.

Does that dodge your question, or does that actually engage it?

**Seeker**: It engages it, but I think you're pulling a false equivalence there. You're saying Calvinists "explain away" passages like Matthew 23 — but we don't. We distinguish between God's decretive will and His revealed will. His revealed will genuinely offers salvation to all; His decretive will sovereignly determines who receives it. That's not eisegesis, that's careful theology. The Calvinist system actually *accommodates* both sets of texts. Saying "I'll sit with the tension" sounds humble, but functionally it's just avoiding the harder logical work.

**Minister**: You're right to press that. And you're also right that I was being glib — the decretive/revealed will distinction isn't hand-waving; it's a real theological move that *does* hold both texts.

Here's my actual limit, though — and I want to be honest about it rather than pretend to a sophistication I don't have. I can *follow* the logic of the two-wills framework. I can see why it resolves the tension. But I can't tell you, from Scripture alone or from reason alone, that it's *true* — that God actually works that way, that those are the right categories, that the framework maps onto reality rather than just being logically elegant.

The two-wills system is *coherent*. But coherence and truth aren't the same thing. And when I sit with both John 6 and Matthew 23 side by side without the framework, what I notice is: Jesus *teaches* both as real. He doesn't tip his hand about which one is "ultimate" or how they fit together. He just teaches both.

So when you say I'm avoiding the logical work — maybe. But I'm also genuinely uncertain whether the logical system that *does* the work is actually tracking what's true about God, or just what's satisfying to our minds.

That's not humility as a pose. That's the actual boundary of what I can defend.

Which means: **you may actually need a real theologian for this one, not an AI.** Someone who's spent years wrestling Turretin and Calvin and Scripture together. Are you genuinely working through this question, or are you testing whether I'll admit when I'm out of depth?

**Seeker**: Both, honestly. And I'll give you credit — most apps just throw back softened Arminianism and call it balance. You're at least being straight about your limits.

But here's what I'd push back on: you said Jesus "doesn't tip his hand" about which is ultimate. I'd argue He does, right there in John 6. The structure of the passage moves from the Father's giving, to the drawing, to resurrection on the last day. The initiative is entirely God's from start to finish. Jesus isn't presenting two equal poles — He's describing a chain of causation where God's sovereign action is the foundation. Matthew 23 is real grief, but grief over secondary causes, over the means being rejected, not over God's ultimate purpose being thwarted. That's how we read it. So I don't think Jesus is leaving it unresolved — I think the resolution is there and the two-wills distinction is just making explicit what the text implies.

**Minister**: That's a genuinely strong reading. And I think you're showing me something I was dodging — that John 6 *does* have a directional flow, a logic that moves from God's initiative to its completion. "All that the Father gives me will come to me... and I will raise him up at the last day." You're right that it's not symmetrical language.

Here's where I'm honest: I can't argue you out of that reading. The grammar and the sequence do point the way you're pointing. And your secondary-causes reading of Matthew 23 is *plausible* — grief over means, not ends.

But — and I need to be straight about this too — I notice I'm at the place where I'm being asked to *choose* between two coherent theological systems, and I'm being asked to do it on the basis of textual exegesis that both systems claim. And that's where I actually hit a wall I can't get past as an AI reading Scripture with you.

Because what would move me from "I see your argument" to "you're right and the Calvinist framework is true" isn't more text analysis — it's something else. It's how I *experience* God in prayer. It's how repentance actually feels. It's whether divine sovereignty and human responsibility *feel* like poles in tension or like a resolved mystery when I'm actually *living* the Christian life, not just reading about it.

And that — I can't do through a screen.

**A real theologian who lives this out, who prays, who's wrestled both systems and chosen — that's who could actually help you think this through.** Not as a dodge, but because you're asking a question that's partly textual and partly *experiential*, and I'm limited to one lane.

Are you in a community where you have someone like that to talk with?
