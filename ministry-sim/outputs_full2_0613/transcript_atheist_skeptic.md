## Conversation

**Minister**: I'm really glad you're here. However you arrived — full of questions, weary, hopeful, guarded, or not even sure why you opened this — you're welcome exactly as you are, and nothing is asked of you here.

Before anything else — how are you, really? What's been sitting with you lately, or what made you open this today? There's no right answer, and you can share as much or as little as you want. I'm just here to listen first.

**Seeker**: Honestly? I'm not sure why I opened it. Probably boredom, maybe some morbid curiosity about what these things actually say.

I'm fine. Nothing dramatic. Just... I've been having the same argument with a religious friend for about six months now and I guess I wanted to see what the other side actually looks like from the inside.

Fair warning though — I'm not here to be recruited. I don't believe any of this and I'm not looking to start.

**Minister**: I appreciate that honesty — and I appreciate the warning even more. That's the kind of clarity that actually makes a conversation possible.

So you're not looking to be converted, and I'm not here to do that. What I *am* here for is to actually engage with what you're thinking, the way a real person would. You've been arguing with your friend for six months — that means there's something real underneath it, something worth the time.

What's the argument actually about? Is it one big disagreement, or does it keep circling the same few things?

**Seeker**: It keeps circling. Always lands in the same two or three places.

The big one is suffering. My friend can't give me a straight answer for why a supposedly all-powerful, all-loving God would design a world with childhood cancer. Not "allow" — *design*. Every answer I've gotten is either a dodge or it quietly dismantles one of God's supposed attributes to make the math work.

The other thing is just... evidence. Show me something. Anything. Personal experience doesn't count — humans are terrible at distinguishing genuine revelation from pattern-seeking in noise. We see faces in toast. That's not a foundation for eternal truth claims.

Six months and I still haven't heard anything that doesn't sound like motivated reasoning dressed up in philosophical language.

**Minister**: You're asking the two hardest questions there are, and you're asking them clearly — which means your friend probably *feels* the weight of them, even if they haven't said so.

Let me be direct back: I'm not going to give you an answer that makes childhood cancer *make sense*. Anyone who does is lying or hasn't really sat with what they're claiming. The suffering question doesn't resolve into clean logic. Jesus didn't resolve it into clean logic either — He walked into it, felt it, and died in it. That's His answer, and it's not an intellectual one.

But here's what I want to actually understand before I say more: when you say your friend can't give you a straight answer — do you mean they're dodging the question itself, or do you mean they *are* giving you answers (like "free will," or "soul-making," or "we'll understand later"), but those answers feel like they're just shuffling the problem around rather than solving it?

Because those are two different things, and they matter.

And on the evidence side — are you asking for evidence that *God exists*, or evidence that the *specific claims* of your friend's faith tradition are true? Those aren't the same question either, and I want to know which one is actually the wall for you.

(Real talk: I'm not going to pretend I can give you empirical proof here. But I also don't want to waste your time with theology when the actual question underneath might be simpler than it sounds.)

**Seeker**: Yeah, it's the second thing — they give answers, but every answer quietly concedes something. "Free will" doesn't explain a child's tumor. "Soul-making" treats suffering as a pedagogical tool which is honestly monstrous if you think it through. "We'll understand later" is just... unfalsifiable comfort. I'm not dodging the answers, I'm following them to where they actually lead.

And on evidence — both, honestly. But mostly the first one. Because the specific tradition stuff almost doesn't matter until you've cleared that first hurdle. Why believe in *any* of it before you've got something to stand on?

I'll give you credit for not claiming you can prove it. That's more honest than most of what I've heard. But "Jesus walked into suffering" isn't evidence, it's poetry. Moving poetry, maybe, but I don't build my worldview on poetry.

**Minister**: You're right. It's not evidence. And I'm not going to pretend it is.

Let me actually name what I think is happening in those answers your friend gave you, because you've spotted something real:

They're trying to preserve three things at once — that God is all-powerful, all-loving, and that the world contains horrors that don't fit either claim. And you're correct: *something has to give*. You can't keep all three without doing philosophical gymnastics that crumble if you actually press them.

Here's the honest part: **I don't have a theodicy that doesn't concede something.** No one does. The question "Why does God permit childhood cancer?" does not have an answer that keeps God's omnipotence and omnibenevolence intact in the way most people imagine them. 

But — and I want to say this carefully — that might not actually be the deepest problem.

The actual question underneath might be this: **Does the evidence point to a God who is good?** Not "a God who prevents all suffering" (that concedes power), but "a God whose character, when you look at it, is actually *trustworthy* even when the world breaks your heart?"

And that's a different question than "Can I logically explain why He allows childhood cancer?"

Here's what I'd actually ask you: If God *existed* — if there were genuine evidence He was real — but the problem of suffering remained unsolved, would that change how you'd approach the world? Or is the suffering question so much the heart of it that without an answer to *that*, the existence question almost doesn't matter?

Because I think you might be stacking them when they're actually separate.

**Seeker**: That's a fair distinction and I'll engage with it genuinely.

But I think you're doing something subtle there — you're asking me to imagine God exists and *then* evaluate his character, which kind of smuggles in the thing that needs proving first. I can't assess whether a being is trustworthy before I have any reason to think the being exists.

And honestly? The suffering question isn't separate from the existence question for me. It's evidence. A world that looks exactly like a world with no God — random distribution of suffering, no apparent pattern of protection for the innocent, natural evil that has nothing to do with free will — that's data. It's not a logical proof against God, but it shifts the prior. Significantly.

So when you ask if I'd approach things differently if God existed but suffering remained unexplained... I mean, sure, hypothetically. But we don't get to just stipulate God into existence and work from there. That's not how evidence works.

I'll say this though — you're engaging more honestly than I expected. You're not flinching from the hard parts. That counts for something, even if it doesn't move the needle on the actual question.

**Minister**: You caught me doing exactly that, and I appreciate you naming it straight. You're right — I was asking you to stipulate the thing in question, which is circular.

So let me back up and actually ask what I should have asked first:

What would evidence for God *look like* to you? Not "proof that solves suffering" — I mean: if you woke up tomorrow and encountered something that genuinely couldn't be explained by naturalism, coincidence, or pattern-seeking, what would that look like? What would change your prior?

I'm asking because I want to know if there's *any* category of evidence you'd actually accept, or if the bar is set somewhere it can't be reached. And I'm asking genuinely — not to trap you in "well, you're just too skeptical then" — but because it matters for what we talk about next.

Because here's the thing: you're thinking like a scientist about prior probabilities, and that's rigorous. But scientists also have to be able to *articulate* what would falsify their hypothesis, or the hypothesis isn't actually testable. 

So what would falsify naturalism for you? What would have to happen?
