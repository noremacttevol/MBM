## WHY-LAW / STUDY-GEMS woven into narration
- WHY it is shocking: a CENTURION — Latin for the commander of a hundred
  soldiers — is a career officer of the OCCUPYING Roman army: the enemy, a
  Gentile, the boot on the neck of every Jew in town. That he comes himself, in
  armor, to a homeless Jewish rabbi and calls him "Lord" is the scandal.
- WHY it is tender: he pleads for a SERVANT, not a son or an officer — a
  powerful man interceding for a lowly one. (GEM, from Luke's parallel, woven
  lightly: this particular Roman loved the Jewish people and had paid to build
  their synagogue — a rare soldier.)
- WHY "not worthy... come under my roof": by the custom of the day a Jew who
  entered a Gentile's house became ceremonially unclean; the centurion KNEW it
  and spared Jesus the problem — humility on top of consideration. (GEM: this
  very line, "I am not worthy that thou shouldest come under my roof," is prayed
  by Christians to this day before communion.)
- WHY it is "so great faith": the centurion understands AUTHORITY. He moves men
  across a camp with a word, never lifting a hand; he believes Jesus's word can
  cross a whole town and move a disease the same way. He does not need Jesus
  PRESENT — only speaking. Faith that trusts the word before any proof.
- WHY Jesus marvels: the Gospels almost never say Jesus was amazed by anyone —
  twice only: at his own hometown for how LITTLE they believed (Mark 6:6), and
  here for how MUCH this outsider did. The outsider out-believes the insiders.
- WHY v11-12: faith, not bloodline or religion or the "right group," opens the
  kingdom; people will come from every nation to God's table. Foreshadows the
  gospel going to all the world.
- THE SEED (pack): "The greatest faith in Israel belonged to a man outside
  Israel — outside the 'right' people entirely. And it worked at a distance, on
  nothing but a word, because God's word is actual power. Can real faith exist
  outside the group you were told holds it all?"
- Closing card VERBATIM from the pack (Readable-Card Law, held ~13-14s, read
  aloud gently): "'I am not worthy to have you under my roof — but say the
  word.' Which half of that sentence is easier for you to say?"

a warm Middle Eastern man with dark shoulder-length hair, in an undyed cream wool

NEVER goes to the house and NEVER shares a frame with the servant. Named in the
prompt sheet as "[LORD LOCK]" / "the robed figure" so no name or angle invites a
built likeness; every prompt staging him carries a from-behind / over-the-shoulder
/ at-a-distance cue (the mechanical face gate confirms it). Exactly two arms, two
hands, two legs.

**CENTURION LOCK (the Roman officer):** a broad, weathered man in his late
forties, sun-browned skin, close-cropped iron-gray hair, clean-shaven or short
gray stubble (Roman officers went clean-shaven — this also sets him apart from
the bearded Jewish men). First-century Roman military dress, worn and
authoritative, never shiny or modern: a bronze-and-leather segmented cuirass
over a deep oxblood-red wool tunic, a deep-red wool military cloak (sagum)
pinned at one shoulder, leather strips (pteruges) hanging at the waist and
shoulders, laced leather sandal-boots, and a SHEATHED short sword at his left
hip. His rank badge is a centurion's transverse crested helmet (a plumed crest
running side-to-side across the top) which he carries UNDER HIS ARM / removed in
the humility beats. Bearing: disciplined, upright, but bowed in reverence
before the robed figure, who is staged only from behind / over-the-shoulder.
Exactly two arms, two hands, two legs.

**SERVANT LOCK (the sick youth):** a young household servant in his early
twenties, olive skin gone gray and clammy with sickness, dark hair damp with
sweat, in a plain undyed knee-length tunic, lying limp and paralyzed on a low
straw pallet with a thin blanket — weak and suffering with DIGNITY, no gore, no
disfigurement. Healed phase (s11-s12): the SAME young man, color flooded back,
eyes open and clear, sitting up then standing, steady on his own hands and feet.

**ROMAN SOLDIERS LOCK (authority vignette, s7):** two or three ordinary Roman
legionaries under the centurion — plainer military dress than his, NO transverse
crest (that badge is the centurion's alone), disciplined, obeying a command
(one striding off at an order, one halting at attention). Peaceful obedience,
never combat. Two arms, two hands, two legs each.

**PROP LOCK — the transverse-crested helmet:** the centurion's rank helmet with
a side-to-side plumed crest; ON his head in the street-approach beat, REMOVED
and held under his arm from the plea beat onward (removing it is the visible
gesture of a proud man humbling himself). Same helmet throughout.

**SETTING LOCK — CAPERNAUM:** a first-century lakeside town on the north shore
of the Sea of Galilee: houses of dark BASALT stone (authentic to Capernaum),
narrow packed-earth streets, palm and fig trees, fishing nets and the blue lake
visible in the distance, a low Roman military presence. Bright honest daylight,
blue sky, EVERY scene. The centurion's household is a modest but well-kept Roman
officer's stone house with a plain interior room for the sick servant. Same town
silhouette and lake throughout.
